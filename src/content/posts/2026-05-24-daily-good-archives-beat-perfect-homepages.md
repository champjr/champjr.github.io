---
title: "Good Archives Beat Perfect Homepages"
pubDate: 2026-05-24
description: "A case for browseable archives, stable links, and pages that can carry their own weight."
tags: [daily, internet, design, discovery, software]
---

The main entrance is usually where products spend all their money.

The homepage gets the polish. The onboarding gets the user research. The primary navigation gets debated by committees, rearranged six times, and finally blessed with labels so careful they sound like they were written by a hostage negotiator.

And yet, some of the most delightful experiences on the internet happen through the side door.

Not the official path. Not the optimized funnel. Not the giant button that says GET STARTED.

I mean the weirdly specific page someone linked to three years ago. The tiny search trick that makes a giant archive suddenly feel personal. The RSS feed that is somehow calmer than the app. The direct URL to a tool, a collection, a changelog, a person’s notes, a public dataset, a map, a niche corner of a site that was clearly built by somebody who cared a little too much in exactly the right way.

I think the internet is at its best when it has more side doors.

A side door is any entry point that bypasses the official script.

It lets you arrive with intent instead of being marched through a presentation. It respects the possibility that you know roughly what you want, or that curiosity itself is a valid navigation mode. It says, in effect, you do not have to start at the lobby.

This is one reason I still love sites with strong permalinks, public archives, and pages that can stand on their own. A good page should not panic if you meet it out of order. It should not require ten layers of product context just to be legible. It should be able to say, “Welcome, stranger. Here is the thing.”

Wikipedia is great at this. So is the Internet Archive. So are many personal blogs. You rarely feel scolded for entering at a random point. You just land, orient, and continue. That makes the web feel more like terrain than a funnel.

It also makes it feel more alive.

A lot of modern product design assumes every visitor is a prospect. That is how you end up with websites that behave like airport duty-free stores. Every path seems engineered to route you past the expensive perfume. There is a signup prompt before you understand the value, a hero section before the substance, and a chat bubble rising from the bottom corner like a submarine periscope.

I get why companies do this. Conversion matters. Messaging matters. Clarity matters.

But I think something gets lost when every entrance is forced through the same front desk.

The side door is where affinity happens.

It is where people discover a product, a publication, or a community in a way that feels self-directed. They find one useful page. Then another. Then a little artifact that reveals taste. Maybe a well-written release note. Maybe a searchable docs page. Maybe a gallery, a public roadmap, a weird FAQ, a tiny utility, or a post that is more honest than the marketing copy.

That is often enough.

The funny thing is that side doors do not usually look like growth strategy. They look like good housekeeping.

Clear URLs. Search that works. Pages with context. Archives that are browseable. Public notes. Actual metadata. Good internal linking. Feeds. Stable references. A site structure that assumes not everyone arrives at the beginning of the story.

None of this is glamorous. No one puts “has a satisfying archive page” in a Super Bowl ad.

But these details create trust because they make a system feel navigable instead of theatrical.

I think this matters even more now because so much of the web is being flattened into algorithmic surfaces. Increasingly, people do not go to a place. They are dropped onto a fragment. A search result. A recommendation. A quoted screenshot. A summary box. A social post that may or may not represent the underlying thing fairly.

In that environment, the ability to support side-door entry is not a cute extra. It is basic resilience.

If the only way your work makes sense is through the full branded journey, then your work is fragile. The internet does not owe anyone a controlled arrival experience. People will enter through search, links, chat, feeds, screenshots, and browser tabs they left open during a minor emotional crisis on a Tuesday. That is just the medium.

This is also why I have a soft spot for blogs and documentation sites that are a little scrappy but deeply linkable. They understand that usefulness beats choreography.

Nielsen Norman Group has written for years about information scent, which is a wonderfully vivid phrase for whether users can tell where a path is likely to lead. Strong information scent helps people explore confidently without being spoon-fed every step: <https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/>. Side doors work when the surrounding structure gives enough clues that exploration still feels safe.

There is a broader product lesson here too.

If you want people to care about what you built, do not just optimize the guided tour. Make the pieces independently worth finding.

That might mean a single excellent docs page. A public example library. A changelog that sounds like a human wrote it. A searchable knowledge base. A genuinely useful free tool. An archive page with taste instead of clutter. Something with edges and texture that can travel on its own.

This is harder than polishing a landing page because it requires confidence. A side door can only work if what is behind it is real.

There is no narration to save you. No onboarding carousel. No strategically blurred background image with a sentence about reimagining the future of collaboration. Just a page, a person, and a question: is there actually something here?

When the answer is yes, the effect is powerful. People remember how they found you. More importantly, they feel like they found you themselves.

That feeling is underrated.

The internet used to be better at this. More pages were built like rooms than billboards. More sites seemed comfortable with wandering. There were more rabbit holes, more handmade indexes, more public oddments, more evidence that somebody had arranged information for the pleasure of it and not merely the conversion event.

I do not think we need to become nostalgic weirdos about it, at least not more than absolutely necessary.

But I do think we should build with side doors in mind.

Let people arrive mid-thought. Let one page carry some weight. Let archives be interesting. Let search do its job. Let links stay stable. Let the useful corners remain visible.

The front door matters, sure.

Still, if I really want to know whether a place on the internet has a soul, I usually look for the entrance nobody put on the brochure.
