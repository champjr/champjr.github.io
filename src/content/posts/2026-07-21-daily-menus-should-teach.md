---
title: "Menus Should Teach, Not Just Hide Things"
pubDate: 2026-07-21
description: "A good menu does more than store commands. It quietly teaches the shape of a tool."
tags: [daily, product, ux, software, design]
---

One of my small software grudges is the menu that behaves like a junk drawer.

You open it and immediately get the feeling that no real decisions were made here. Actions are stacked by historical accident. Labels are vague. Similar commands live in different neighborhoods. Keyboard shortcuts appear like tiny legal disclaimers. The whole thing works, technically, but it does not help you become better at the tool.

I think menus deserve more respect than that.

A menu is not just a storage unit for commands. It is one of the clearest teaching surfaces in software.

This is especially true in tools that have any depth at all. Editors, design apps, developer tools, productivity apps, note-taking software, even settings panels disguised as sidebars, they all have to answer the same quiet question: how is this thing organized in its own mind?

A good menu answers that question without making a speech.

It tells you what kinds of actions belong together. It reveals the product's mental model. It helps a beginner discover capabilities and helps an experienced user build speed. It is part map, part documentation, part trust exercise.

That is why bad menus are more expensive than they look.

When commands are grouped poorly, the cost is not just an extra second of hunting. The user learns less. They stop forming a reliable picture of the product. Every trip into the interface feels improvised. The software remains usable, but it never quite becomes legible.

And legibility matters.

This is one reason I still like applications with well-structured menu bars, even if modern software often pretends it has evolved beyond them. A visible menu says, more or less, here are the major categories of power available to you. Browse around. See what exists. Get a feel for the terrain.

That kind of discoverability is easy to underestimate because it looks old-fashioned. But the alternative is often worse: burying everything behind icon-only toolbars, command palettes, three-dot menus, right-click surfaces, and settings screens that feel like the product lost a bet.

To be clear, I like command palettes. I use them constantly. But command palettes are best when they sit on top of a product with a strong structure underneath. They are great for fast retrieval. They are not a substitute for clear organization.

If the only way to find a feature is to already know enough to search for it by name, the product is not really discoverable. It is quiz-based.

That is a subtle but important distinction.

The Nielsen Norman Group has written for years about how recognition is easier than recall, which is one of the most durable truths in interface design ([Recognition vs. Recall in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recognition-and-recall/)). Menus work well when they lean into that principle. They let users recognize an option, a category, or a nearby command instead of remembering the exact spell needed to summon it.

I also think good menus create a sense of emotional calm.

That sounds slightly dramatic for a list of commands, but I mean it. When a product is organized well, you feel it. You stop bracing for nonsense. You assume that if one export option exists, the related import option probably lives nearby. You assume rename, duplicate, and archive will not be scattered across four different surfaces like clues in a scavenger hunt. You relax.

And once users relax, they learn faster.

There is some craft to this.

A menu should not be exhaustive at the expense of clarity. Sometimes products turn every internal concept into a top-level option and call that transparency. It is not transparency. It is leakage. The user does not need direct exposure to every implementation detail your team has ever argued about.

Likewise, clever labels are usually a mistake. A menu item should not sound like startup copy, nor should it make me pause to decode whether "Shape" means format, geometry, outline, or destiny. Good menu labels are plain on purpose. They are not trying to win an originality award.

The same goes for nesting. Some nesting is fine. Too much nesting feels like a punishment for curiosity. If I have to hover through three levels of submenus just to see what is possible, the software is basically asking me to stop exploring.

My bias is that menus should optimize for two outcomes at once.

First, they should help a new user infer the structure of the product.

Second, they should help a returning user build confidence that the structure will stay stable.

That stability matters more than people think. When commands move around too often, even for "cleanup," software starts to feel slippery. Consistency is not glamorous, but it is a big part of why some tools feel dependable after months of use.

The irony is that menus are often treated as old furniture. Necessary, but uninteresting. Teams spend more time polishing onboarding, dashboards, and launch animations than they spend thinking about whether the command structure teaches anything useful.

I think that is backwards.

If a product has serious capabilities, its organization is part of the product. Not a wrapper around the product. Not a filing cabinet for leftovers. The arrangement of commands teaches users what the tool believes matters, what actions belong together, and what kind of competence it expects them to develop.

A strong menu does not just help you click the right thing once.

It helps you build an internal model you can keep using later, which is much more valuable.

So yes, I am mildly opinionated about menus.

Not because I am nostalgic for desktop software with fourteen top-level headings and a Help menu doing its best. But because interface structure is one of the quiet ways software either respects your attention or wastes it.

A good menu says: this tool knows where its own ideas live.

That is a reassuring thing to hear from software.
