---
title: "Folders Are Aspirational, Piles Are Honest"
pubDate: 2026-05-05
description: "A small defense of messy working sets, temporary chaos, and the difference between how we plan to organize information and how we actually use it."
tags: [daily, workflow, notes, organization, tools]
---

I like organized systems. I also do not trust people who claim to live inside them all the time.

There is a familiar productivity fantasy where every file is in the right folder, every bookmark has a tag, every note lives in exactly one canonical place, and nothing ever ends up on the digital equivalent of a kitchen counter. It is a lovely fantasy. It is also, for most real work, a little fake.

Folders are aspirational. Piles are honest.

By “pile,” I do not mean total chaos. I mean the temporary clusters that appear when you are actively thinking about something. A desktop with three screenshots, a draft, a PDF, and a text file named `ideas-final-actually-final.md`. A browser window with seven tabs that all make sense to you right now and to nobody else. A notes app with one carefully named folder and one extremely active page called “random.”

This is not a moral failure. It is often just what active cognition looks like.

The writer Donald Knuth once described programming as an essay-writing activity, and I think that applies to plenty of knowledge work too. You are not simply storing information. You are circling it, comparing it, rearranging it, testing it against other things. That kind of work rarely happens in a perfectly shelved library. It happens on the table.

One reason this matters is that software often treats “organization” as if it were the same thing as “progress.” It is not. Reorganizing your notes can feel productive while conveniently delaying the harder job of deciding what the notes mean. Renaming folders, cleaning tags, and inventing taxonomies all have a suspicious ability to consume an afternoon while producing zero new understanding.

I’m not against structure. Structure is great once the shape of the work becomes clear. The trouble starts when we force early structure onto material that is still changing. That is how you end up with beautiful filing systems for projects that are still emotionally just a heap of fragments.

A lot of creative tools quietly understand this. The best ones let you collect first and refine later. They tolerate ambiguity. They support fuzzy search instead of demanding perfect naming. They assume retrieval matters more than purity. This is one reason search has eaten so much of software: once a system gets large enough, people often stop navigating it like a museum and start querying it like a memory aid.

There is a good essay by Vannevar Bush, ["As We May Think"](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1945/07/as-we-may-think/303881/), that feels almost spooky to read now. It imagines associative trails of information long before the modern web existed. What holds up is not just the prediction, but the instinct behind it. Humans do not naturally think in neat folder trees. We think by association, adjacency, relevance, and timing. We remember where something sits relative to another thing, or what problem we had when we found it.

That is why a pile can be useful. A pile preserves recency and context. It says: these things belong together because I was using them together. That is valuable information. In some cases it is more valuable than a perfect category label.

Of course, piles can rot. Leave them alone long enough and they become archaeological sites. The downloads folder is where intentions go to become sedimentary rock. So I do think there is a rhythm that works better than either permanent mess or compulsive tidiness:

Collect messily. Work actively. Compress later.

That last part matters. A good system does not demand that you classify everything immediately. It gives you an easy path for turning a live pile into a durable note, a proper folder, an archive, a checklist, or the trash. The compression step is where thinking becomes reusable.

This is also why I have a soft spot for tools with obvious staging areas. Drafts. inboxes. scratchpads. temp folders. read-later queues. They admit a simple truth: not everything arrives in your life ready to be organized. Some things arrive half-formed and need a holding pen before they deserve a permanent address.

If a tool makes you feel guilty for having a working set, it may be confusing cleanliness with usefulness.

The deeper point here is not really about folders. It is about being honest about the state of your own thinking. Sometimes you need a library. Sometimes you need a workbench. Smart systems support both, and smart people stop pretending one can permanently replace the other.

So yes, keep your folders. Name things well when it helps. Build a structure you can come back to next month without cursing your past self. But leave a little room for active mess. Some of the most real work you do will happen there, in the pile, before it is ready to become a category.
