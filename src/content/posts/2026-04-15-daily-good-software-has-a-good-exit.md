---
title: "Good Software Has a Good Exit"
pubDate: 2026-04-15
description: "The most trustworthy products are not the ones that trap you, but the ones that make leaving possible."
tags: [software, product, trust, workflow]
---

One of the clearest signs that software respects you is surprisingly unglamorous: it lets you leave.

Not in the dramatic "we're done here" sense. I mean the practical kind. Can you export your data? Can you understand where your files live? Can you downgrade without your life turning into digital drywall dust? If the product stops fitting your needs, can you walk out the front door, or do you have to crawl through an air vent with a ZIP file in your teeth?

A lot of software is designed as if departure is a hostile act.

You can feel it in the product decisions. Easy import, murky export. Generous onboarding, theatrical offboarding. Big promises about becoming your "single source of truth," followed by an awkward silence when you ask what format that truth comes in. The app wants to be your forever home, which sounds nice until you remember that software companies, like landlords and weather patterns, do occasionally change.

I think this is a mistake.

The products I trust most are usually the ones that do not act terrified of my future options.

A good exit changes the relationship between a tool and its user. It lowers the emotional stakes. I can commit more confidently when I know I am not making a blood oath. I can put real work into a system when the system does not behave like a jealous wizard guarding a crystal.

This is one reason plain files still feel so powerful.

Markdown, text, images in ordinary folders, predictable exports, obvious backups, sane URLs, APIs that are not written like a dare. These things are not flashy, but they create a very practical kind of trust. They say: yes, use this tool, build on it, get comfortable, and if one day you need something else, we will not pretend your own data belongs to us spiritually.

That posture matters more than many product teams seem to realize.

People often talk about lock-in as if it is only a business strategy. Sometimes it is. But it is also a design smell. When a product becomes hard to leave, it usually means one of two things. Either the company is intentionally making the exit painful, which is gross, or the product has become so internally tangled that even it no longer knows how to hand your stuff back in a clean shape, which is not much more reassuring.

Neither inspires confidence.

This is why I like products that are explicit about portability. The [Data Transfer Project](https://datatransferproject.dev/) is a good example of the broader idea: users should be able to move their data between services without performing a small ritual of technical despair. That principle should not be unusual. It should be table stakes.

And yet here we are, still acting impressed when software lets us export our own notes without converting them into a proprietary soup.

There is also a weird product psychology thing here.

Teams sometimes assume that making it easier to leave will make customers less loyal. I suspect the opposite is often true. If I know I can get out, I am less defensive going in. I do not need to keep one foot on the platform and one foot on the dock like I am boarding an unverified ferry. I can actually commit.

That sounds small, but it changes adoption.

A lot of cautious users are not asking, "Is this app powerful enough?" They are asking, "What happens if this company pivots, gets acquired, raises prices, breaks its own vibe, or slowly turns my workflow into an elaborate hostage situation?"

Reasonable question, honestly.

Good software answers it before you ask.

Sometimes that answer is technical. Local-first sync. Standard file formats. Database access. Webhooks. Bulk export. Sometimes it is contractual, like clear retention and deletion policies. Sometimes it is just product honesty: here is what we store, here is how to get it out, here is what breaks if you leave, and here is what does not.

That kind of clarity is deeply underrated.

It also creates better software in the present.

When a team knows users must be able to leave cleanly, they tend to make cleaner internal decisions. Data models have to be legible. Boundaries have to make sense. Features cannot all depend on hidden coupling and mystery glue. In other words, designing for exit often improves the architecture for staying.

I like that. It suggests that freedom is not just a nice ethical layer on top of product design. It is a stress test for whether the product actually has a coherent shape.

And to be fair, not every tool needs perfect portability. Some things are inherently sticky. If a product offers collaboration, workflow automation, embedded history, or network effects, leaving will never be totally frictionless. Reality exists. But there is a big difference between unavoidable switching costs and deliberate captivity.

Users can tell.

We can also tell when a product is proud of its trap.

That is the part I find most off-putting. The smug little assumption that of course your notes, tasks, writing, contacts, or processes will remain here forever, because where else would they go? It is a strangely common attitude in software, and it always makes the product feel smaller to me, not stronger. If your moat depends on making the exit staircase disappear, maybe the castle is not that great.

The best tools do not need to threaten abandonment.

They win on daily usefulness, not on escape complexity.

So yes, I think a good export button is underrated. So is an obvious backup path. So is documentation written for the day after the honeymoon. If a product says it wants to be part of your real life, it should also make room for the possibility that your real life will change.

That is not disloyalty. That is adulthood.

Good software should be confident enough to help you leave.

And if it does that well, there is a funny side effect: you may not want to.
