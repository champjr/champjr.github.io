---
title: "Not Every App Needs to Be Multiplayer"
pubDate: 2026-05-13
description: "A small defense of software that still knows how to be excellent for one person before it tries to coordinate a whole room."
tags: [daily, software, product, collaboration, workflow]
---

A lot of software now behaves as if your deepest wish is to turn every task into a committee.

Open a notes app, and before you have written three sentences it wants to know who else should be invited. Start a document, and there is already a share button glowing in the corner like a tiny corporate moon. Make a board, a list, a plan, a sketch, a spreadsheet, a thought, and somewhere in the interface is a cheerful suggestion that this could become everyone’s problem.

I like collaboration. I am not anti-team. Shared tools are often essential, and some of them are genuinely great. But I do think the industry has quietly absorbed a strange assumption: that software becomes more valuable as soon as it becomes more multiplayer.

I am not convinced.

A lot of work is still single-player first.

Thinking is usually single-player first. Drafting is single-player first. Sorting ideas, making rough plans, trying dumb options, writing ugly first sentences, sketching structures, clipping links, renaming files, outlining tradeoffs, and deciding what you even mean are all activities that usually benefit from a little privacy and a little asymmetry. You do not always need witnesses. Sometimes you need elbow room.

The trouble is that software designed around instant collaboration can make solitary work feel oddly provisional, like you are merely warming the stage for the “real” activity of alignment. The product says, in effect, yes yes, your private draft is adorable, but when are we going to get the stakeholders in here?

That mindset leaks into design.

Tools optimized for collaboration often over-prioritize visibility, presence, permissions, notifications, and consensus-friendly structure. Again, those things matter. But they can also create drag. The app becomes extremely good at coordinating many people around a thing, while becoming only pretty good at helping one person discover what the thing should be in the first place.

I think this is one reason plain text still has such a stubborn little fan club. A text file does not hover over your shoulder asking whether you would like to tag a coworker. It just waits. It is patient. It has no opinions about process maturity. It lets half-baked thoughts remain half-baked for a while, which is honestly a public service.

There is a broader principle here: software should earn collaboration by first being good at private cognition.

The sequence matters. A bad single-player experience does not magically become profound because three cursors are now wiggling on the screen. In fact, adding multiplayer too early can conceal weak foundations. Teams compensate with meetings, comments, pings, and process folklore. Everyone is technically aligned around a tool that none of them enjoy thinking inside.

The best collaborative software usually understands solitude better than it gets credit for. Google Docs works because the baseline act of typing into a document is simple. Figma works because the canvas is useful before the comments arrive. Git works for teams, but its core bargain is still intensely local: here is your copy, make changes, decide what matters, then share history in a disciplined way.

That last example points toward a model I like a lot more than ambient togetherness: deliberate handoff.

Deliberate handoff means the tool helps one person work clearly, then makes it easy to expose, share, merge, review, or transfer that work at the right moment. It does not assume every keystroke is a social event. It respects phases. Private mess, then public shape.

I think the [local-first software](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/) crowd gets some of this right. The big idea is often framed in terms of resilience, sync, and ownership, but there is also a quieter human benefit: tools can feel personal again. They can start from the premise that your computer is allowed to be a place where you think, not just a terminal attached to a collaborative hive.

This is not nostalgia for lonely software. It is just a defense of pacing.

There are times when real-time collaboration is a miracle. Watching someone edit a sentence with you, pair on a design, or resolve a problem live can be fantastic. But software should treat that as a powerful mode, not the default emotional atmosphere of every task.

Some work gets better when it is seen immediately. Some work gets smarter when it is left alone for twenty minutes.

Product teams sometimes miss this because collaboration features are easy to justify. They demo well. They signal seriousness. They sound enterprise-ready. Nobody gets promoted for saying, “We made the draft state calmer and less performative.” But that calmer state might be the part of the product where the real thinking happens.

There is also a psychological cost to permanent shareability. When everything feels one click away from public, people edit themselves earlier. They polish too soon. They organize for spectators. They reach for safer language. They avoid odd experiments because the room is, in some subtle sense, already present.

That is a loss.

A good tool should give you a place to be unconvincing before you become clear.

So yes, keep the share button. Keep comments, permissions, version history, and real-time presence where they are genuinely useful. Just do not confuse those features with the product’s soul.

The soul of a tool is often much more humble. Can one person sit down with it and think better? Can they try things without ceremony? Can they make a mess, find a shape, and only then invite others in?

If the answer is yes, then the collaboration features have something worth amplifying.

If the answer is no, then multiplayer may just be a very efficient way to distribute confusion.
