---
title: "Every App Needs a Scratchpad"
pubDate: 2026-05-26
description: "Why lightweight, in-context note space is one of the most underrated product features."
tags: [product, software, ux, notes]
---

There is a category of software feature that sounds trivial until you live without it for a month.

A scratchpad is one of those features.

By scratchpad, I mean a small place inside a tool where you can temporarily think. Not publish. Not formalize. Not convert into a blessed artifact with a title, tags, metadata, and workflow status. Just think. Jot a few lines. Paste a weird URL. Keep a half-formed checklist. Leave yourself a note that says "come back to this after lunch" and then actually find it later.

A surprising number of modern apps are hostile to this kind of thinking.

They are very good at structured work. They love objects. Create a task. Create a document. Create a record. Create a ticket. Create a board. Attach it to a project. Assign an owner. Add a due date. Select a color. Invite a teammate. Choose whether this item is backlog, in progress, blocked, or complete.

And sometimes all I want is a napkin.

That sounds like a small distinction, but I think it matters a lot. Many people do not move from thought to formal object in one clean step. They move from vague feeling, to rough note, to useful shape, and only then into something structured. Software that skips that middle stage ends up making people do the cognitive translation work in their heads, or in some other app.

That is how you get the classic modern workflow: a task manager for official commitments, a notes app for messy thinking, a text file for temporary glue, a screenshot folder full of accidental memory, and a browser with twenty tabs acting as a guilty little holding pen.

None of this is catastrophic. It is just expensive in tiny ways.

Every context switch has a tax. Open another app. Find the right note. Decide where this thought "belongs." Rename it because blank untitled notes breed like rabbits. Then return to the original app and reconstitute the idea you were holding in your head three minutes ago. Software rarely feels bad because of one giant failure. It often feels bad because it keeps charging these tiny taxes.

This is why I think more apps should ship with a built-in scratchpad. Not a heavyweight wiki. Not a collaboration suite hiding inside a settings menu. Just a low-pressure place to offload working memory.

Good developer tools already understand this instinct. The terminal is full of informal space: shell history, temporary files, commented commands, README notes to future-you. Jupyter notebooks became popular partly because they let people mix code, output, and thought without prematurely deciding what the final artifact should be. Tools like [marimo](https://marimo.io/) are interesting in part because they treat exploration as a first-class activity instead of a messy prelude you are supposed to clean up later.

I think product teams outside developer tooling should take that lesson seriously.

Imagine a calendar app with a real scratch area attached to each day, not just formal events. Imagine a shopping app where you could keep temporary notes while comparing products. Imagine project software with a per-project blotter for fragments that are not ready to become tasks. Imagine a design tool with a tiny sidecar note space for observations, not just comments pinned to pixels like little landmines.

Some software sort of has this already, but often in an accidental or awkward way. People misuse description fields, create fake draft tasks, or open a chat with themselves because the product failed to provide a sanctioned place for incomplete thoughts. Users are extremely good at inventing workarounds. That does not mean the product is meeting them where they are.

There is also a philosophical angle here. A scratchpad signals something about how a tool sees its users. It says: you are allowed to be mid-thought here. You are allowed to not know the final form yet. You are allowed to use this tool while thinking, not only after thinking.

That is a more generous model of computing.

The opposite model is strangely moralizing. It suggests the user should arrive organized, certain, and ready to classify everything correctly on first contact. That is nonsense. Most worthwhile work starts as a blur. The right software does not punish the blur. It gives it somewhere to land.

Of course, scratchpads can become junk drawers. That is the risk. But junk drawers are useful. The problem is not that they exist. The problem is when every drawer in the house becomes a junk drawer because there is nowhere obvious to put the in-between stuff.

A good scratchpad feature would solve for that with a few simple rules: make it easy to find, easy to clear, and easy to promote into something more formal when the time is right. The promotion step is important. A scratchpad should not be a dead end. It should be a staging area.

I suspect a lot of software would feel dramatically better if it asked one humble product question during design: where does the half-baked thought go?

If the answer is "somewhere else," the tool is probably outsourcing an important part of the job.
