---
title: "More Software Should Have a Half-Life"
pubDate: 2026-05-31
description: "A lot of digital clutter survives not because it matters, but because products never learned how to let it fade."
tags: [daily, software, product, notes, design]
---

A surprising amount of software is bad at endings.

It is decent at creating things, decent at syncing them, and sometimes even decent at helping you search through the wreckage later. But it is terrible at letting low-value stuff fade away with dignity.

So we get apps full of immortal clutter.

Old notifications that still look vaguely actionable. Drafts from three ideas ago. Bookmarks for articles we meant to read during a previous era of optimism. Documents whose main job is now just proving that we once had a plan.

I think more software should have a half-life.

Not hard deletion by default. Not some cheerful minimalist regime where the app throws away your life because it recently discovered "calm technology" and got a little too excited. I mean a product philosophy where information can become less prominent, less sticky, and less interruptive as it ages and fails to prove its relevance.

Physical spaces do this naturally. Receipts sink into bags. Sticky notes lose their adhesive. Newspapers become fish-wrapper, metaphorically if not literally. Time is a ruthless curator in the real world.

Digital systems, by contrast, tend to preserve everything with the emotional intelligence of a warehouse.

This is partly because storage got cheap. In 2011, Marc Andreessen famously wrote that [software is eating the world](https://www.wsj.com/articles/SB10001424053111903480904576512250915629460). A less glamorous sequel is that software also keeps eating our leftovers. Once an app can store one more item at near-zero cost, it has almost no built-in reason to help that item gracefully recede.

The result is not just clutter. It is a weird flattening of significance.

If every note, task, notification, and saved item remains equally present forever, then nothing gets the benefit of distance. The grocery list from Tuesday can sit next to the genuinely important idea you have been circling for months. An "FYI" notification from a random integration can hang around with the same visual posture as something you actually needed to act on.

Software often mistakes preservation for respect.

I do not think that is respect. I think it is avoidance.

A respectful system should help distinguish between what is still alive and what is merely still stored.

Email, for all its sins, accidentally understands some of this. Newer messages naturally rise. Older ones sink unless revived. The inbox is still chaotic, sure, but at least time has consequences. Meanwhile a lot of newer apps act like every artifact deserves permanent front-row seating just because it exists.

This is how tools become background stress machines. They are not overwhelming because any single item is dramatic. They are overwhelming because the interface refuses to admit that most things decay.

There are good examples in the wild, and I wish more products copied them.

Read-it-later apps sometimes resurface neglected articles instead of just hoarding them. Task apps often distinguish "due now" from "someday maybe," which is one of the great acts of mercy in software. Even operating systems quietly archive or deprioritize things all the time. Gmail's Priority Inbox, whether or not you like it, is based on the same basic idea: not everything should shout at the same volume forever.

This is also why I like features such as auto-archiving completed tasks, expiring temporary links, or notes that can deliberately go dormant until they are touched again. These are not anti-user features. They are anti-stagnation features.

The design challenge is that people hear "fading" and imagine loss.

But fading is not the same thing as deleting.

Libraries do not burn books because they are shelved.

A good half-life model would preserve retrieval while reducing ambient noise. Old items could remain searchable, but lose default prominence. Unused dashboards could collapse. Ancient tabs could get gently grouped into "probably historical artifacts." Notes that have not been opened in a year could shift into a cooler visual layer, like the app is politely saying, "I kept this, but I stopped putting it in your face."

Honestly, I want more software that understands the difference between memory and attention.

Those are not the same resource.

Storage is memory. Interface real estate is attention. Search indexes are memory. Notification badges are attention. Backups are memory. Sidebars with forty-seven stale objects are attention. Too many products spend attention as if it were as cheap as storage, and that is how you end up feeling vaguely guilty every time you open an app that was supposed to make your life more organized.

There is a useful parallel here to gardening. A good garden is not just a place where things grow. It is also a place where things get pruned. If nothing ever gets cut back, the healthy plants do not win some noble battle for survival. Everything just turns into a competition for light.

Digital spaces work the same way.

Without pruning, interfaces become overgrown. The oldest stuff is not important, exactly, but it still occupies surface area. It still asks to be scanned. It still contributes to that low-grade "I should probably deal with this" feeling that modern software seems to generate as a side effect.

I suspect the next wave of product quality is not going to come only from smarter creation tools. It is also going to come from better decay mechanics.

Not decay as failure. Decay as design.

Products should ask: what happens if this item is never touched again? Does it sit here forever, cluttering the same view? Does it keep notifying people from beyond the grave? Or does the system know how to age it into the background while keeping it recoverable?

That is a much more humane question than teams usually ask.

The default software instinct is to help us accumulate. Save this. Keep that. Pin this. Add a tab. Capture the thought. Duplicate the board. Start a draft. Open a loop. The problem is not any one of those actions. The problem is that very few products are equally serious about closure, dormancy, and graceful irrelevance.

And if software is going to keep helping us collect pieces of our lives, it should also learn how to compost.
