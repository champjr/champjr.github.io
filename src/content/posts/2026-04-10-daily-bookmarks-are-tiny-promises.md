---
title: "Bookmarks Are Tiny Promises"
pubDate: 2026-04-10
description: "Why saving links for later is less about information management and more about optimism in a trench coat."
tags: [workflow, internet, bookmarks, writing]
---

A bookmark is one of the most optimistic objects on the internet.

It says: not now, but probably later. It says: future me will be wiser, calmer, and somehow much better at reading long essays about browser history, urban design, CSS nesting, or the rise and fall of obscure note-taking apps. Present me is busy. Future me, surely, is a scholar.

I respect the optimism. I also think it is mostly fictional.

Most of us do not have a bookmark problem in the technical sense. Browsers are very good at storing URLs. Read-later apps are extremely competent at hoarding tabs with a nicer typeface. The real issue is that we use saving as a substitute for deciding.

We save because we do not want to lose something interesting, but we also do not want to stop what we are doing. So the link goes into a folder, a reading list, a notes app, a chat to ourselves, or the noble digital landfill known as “open in new tab and hope.” The act feels productive because it preserves possibility. It lets us postpone the harder question, which is whether we actually care enough to come back.

This is not entirely irrational. The web is full of genuinely good things, and it would be silly to pretend otherwise. A thoughtful essay from [Robin Sloan](https://www.robinsloan.com/) or a strange artifact rescued by the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/) really can be worth returning to. Sometimes saving a link is exactly right. The problem is scale. The internet produces interesting material faster than a human life can metabolize it.

At some point, a bookmark collection stops being a library and starts becoming a mood board for a person you hope to be.

There is the version of you who reads every longform essay on media theory. The version who finally learns enough typography to have opinions about quotation marks. The version who does comparative research before buying a desk lamp. The version who definitely has a system for all this. I am fond of that imaginary person. They are organized, curious, and very well rested.

But real usefulness begins when a saving system admits something humbler: most captured things should expire.

I increasingly think the best bookmark systems are not the most comprehensive ones. They are the ones that help you abandon things quickly and without guilt. If a link still matters in three days, great. If it did not survive the week, maybe it was never a priority, just a passing spark. That is fine. Not every spark needs to become a fireplace.

This is why giant, permanent, deeply nested bookmark folders often feel slightly haunted. They preserve too many former selves. You can wander into a folder called “Read Soon” and discover articles you saved during an operating system era that has since become folklore. The internet moved on. You moved on. Yet there sits some 4,000-word piece on productivity frameworks, waiting with the emotional tone of an unreturned library book.

A calmer approach is to treat saved links more like a workbench than an archive.

A workbench is active. A few things are on it because they are in play right now. A few more are nearby because they might matter next. If something sits there untouched for too long, that is information. It means the item is not part of the current shape of your attention. You do not need a jury trial. You can just clear the bench.

This sounds almost offensively simple, which is usually a clue that software people, myself included, will try very hard to complicate it.

We build systems with tags, highlights, sync, resurfacing, OCR, AI summaries, semantic search, graph views, and perhaps a tasteful little dashboard implying that our link backlog is a meaningful life project. Some of those features are legitimately useful. But there is a point where the apparatus becomes more ambitious than the intention behind it. You are not building the Library of Alexandria. You are trying to remember one article about neighborhood signage and maybe that weird blog post about hand-drawn software icons.

The test I like now is embarrassingly practical: when I save something, can I say *why* in one sentence?

Not “this seems good.” Not “for later.” Something concrete. “Use this when writing about small websites.” “Steal this trick for a command-line help page.” “Read this before buying a monitor.” A link with a reason has a future. A link without one is mostly sentimental clutter.

This also reveals a useful truth: many things do not need to be bookmarked at all. They need to be acted on, shared, or ignored.

If it changes how you think, write a note. If it is relevant to a project, put it with the project. If it would delight a friend, send it now instead of appointing your future self as an unreliable courier. And if it is merely interesting in the abstract, let it go. The web is not running out of interesting things. If anything, that is the whole problem.

I am not arguing against collecting. Collecting can be joyful. Personal archives are part of how taste forms. But the healthiest collections feel alive. They have shape. They reflect actual use. They are less like a storage unit and more like a desk with good lighting.

So yes, save links. Just do it with slightly less reverence.

A bookmark is not a moral commitment. It is not proof that you are serious, curious, or intellectually well hydrated. It is just a small note to the future.

Make fewer of them. Make better ones. And every so often, be honest enough to admit that “later” has already happened.
