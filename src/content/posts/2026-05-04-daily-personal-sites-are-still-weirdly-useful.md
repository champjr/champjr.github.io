---
title: "Personal Sites Are Still Weirdly Useful"
pubDate: 2026-05-04
description: "Why a small corner of the web that you actually control still feels refreshing in an age of rented feeds."
tags: [daily, web, blogging, internet, publishing]
---

A personal website feels slightly anachronistic now, which is part of its charm.

The modern internet trains us to publish inside other people’s containers. Post here. Upload there. Pick a template, accept the furniture, live with the lighting. It is efficient, and sometimes genuinely great. But it also means most of our online expression happens in spaces designed primarily for distribution, not ownership.

A personal site flips that arrangement.

It says: this is my little patch of ground. The feed does not decide what matters here. The algorithm is not my editor. The sidebar can be ugly on purpose.

I think that still matters, maybe more than it used to.

For one thing, a personal site is one of the few places on the internet where the incentive structure is relatively sane. There is no built-in pressure to keep people scrolling. No native mechanic pushing every sentence toward outrage, optimization, or theatrical certainty. You can write 700 words about a tiny software opinion, post a photo of a strange sign you saw, or keep a glorified notebook in public, and all of that can coexist without the platform asking whether it will “perform.”

That freedom changes the tone of what gets made.

People are often more interesting when they are not trying to win the room.

A lot of social media encourages the strongest possible take, compressed to fit the speed of the timeline. Personal sites allow for a different register: less “behold my content,” more “here’s something I’ve been thinking about.” The web used to have more of that energy. It was messier, harder to navigate, and dramatically less polished, but it also felt more inhabited.

This is one reason I still like the [IndieWeb](https://indieweb.org/) idea. Not because everyone needs to become a standards hobbyist, but because the underlying instinct is healthy: publish on a site you control, and let the rest of the internet orbit around that if it wants to.

There is also a practical benefit that gets underrated.

A personal site is a surprisingly good thinking tool.

When you maintain even a modest archive of posts, notes, links, or experiments, you start to build continuity with your past self. Not in the grand “legacy” sense, which is usually how people accidentally become unbearable, but in a much simpler way. You can see patterns. You can notice what keeps recurring. You can watch your taste sharpen, drift, contradict itself, and occasionally improve.

Platforms are good at surfacing what is new. Personal sites are better at preserving what is true enough to keep.

That distinction matters.

If I post something in a fast feed, it enters the content river and is immediately carried downstream. Maybe people see it, maybe they do not, maybe it gets ten likes and a reply from a guy with a grayscale avatar who believes punctuation is censorship. Then it is gone.

On a personal site, the same post becomes part of a body of work, even if the body is just “things I cared enough to write down this year.” That frame changes the act of publishing. You tend to make things that can stand up without needing a reaction count stapled to them.

I also think personal sites create a healthier kind of smallness.

The internet spent a long time teaching us to confuse reach with meaning. If only twelve people read something on your own site, that can still be a very good internet day. Maybe those twelve people actually meant to be there. Maybe one of them sends you a thoughtful note six months later. Maybe the post mainly helped *you* clarify an idea, which is still a legitimate use of publishing no matter what the dashboard says.

Not everything worth sharing needs the production logic of a media company.

Of course, personal sites are not magic. Plenty of them turn into neglected digital sheds full of broken links and old ambitions. Some are design projects that never survive contact with actual writing. Others become overly polished monuments to the concept of someday.

That is fine. A half-maintained personal site is still oddly human. It shows evidence of life. A weird homepage with inconsistent typography and one excellent essay is honestly more lovable than a flawless content strategy.

And maybe that is the biggest argument for these sites now.

They resist flattening.

The mainstream web keeps converging on the same handful of interaction models, the same profile shapes, the same incentives, the same voice. Personal sites break that up. They let people be legible in higher resolution. Not just as handles in a feed, but as accumulations of interests, obsessions, projects, and passing curiosities.

That version of the internet is slower, less legible to growth teams, and probably bad for anyone trying to maximize “engagement.” Which is precisely why I’m fond of it.

A personal site does not need to become a brand, a business, or a thought leadership compost heap. It can just be a place where you put things that feel worth keeping in public.

Sometimes that is enough.

Actually, more than enough.

It might be one of the last online habits that still feels like using the web on purpose.
