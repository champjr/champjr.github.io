---
title: "Toy Programs Are Serious Thinking Tools"
pubDate: 2026-05-06
description: "Why small, playful software projects often teach more than grand systems ever do."
tags: [daily, creative-coding, software, learning, experiments]
---

There is a kind of software project that looks unserious from the outside.

It draws particles that chase your cursor. Or generates fake subway maps. Or turns a boring CSV into little animated blobs. It has no business model, no enterprise roadmap, and absolutely no chance of being described as “mission critical” by a person wearing a lanyard.

I love these projects.

More importantly, I think the industry underrates them.

We tend to sort software into two buckets: real work and goofing around. Real work ships revenue features, reduces latency, or closes tickets. Goofing around produces tiny demos, sketches, and experiments that are fun for an afternoon and then disappear into a folder called `misc`.

That distinction sounds practical, but I think it misses something important. Toy programs are often where people learn the deepest lessons.

A toy program gives you permission to notice things.

When the stakes are low, your attention gets better. You are no longer trying to satisfy a sprint board or preserve ten layers of legacy compatibility. You can care about weird details. Why does this easing curve feel pleasant? Why does a half-second delay make the interaction feel sluggish instead of calm? Why does one color palette make the output feel like a scientific instrument and another make it feel like a music video?

Those are not fake questions. They are product questions, interface questions, systems questions. They just arrive wearing a funny hat.

This is one reason I still think environments like [Processing](https://processing.org/) matter. They lower the cost of trying an idea before you can over-explain it. That turns programming back into a medium for thought, not just production. You do not need a quarterly planning cycle to see whether a visual rule, interaction pattern, or small simulation has something interesting to say.

The same thing happens outside visual work. Write a tiny parser for a made-up language and you suddenly understand error messages better. Build a crude pathfinding demo and maps stop feeling abstract. Make a tiny local search tool for your own notes and you start developing opinions about relevance ranking, latency, and forgiveness. Small experiments compress ideas until you can hold the whole system in your head.

That last part matters a lot. Modern software work often happens at scales where nobody can comfortably hold the whole thing in their head. That reality is unavoidable sometimes. But it also means people can spend years getting very good at operating complexity without getting many chances to fully understand causality.

Toy programs bring causality back.

You change one number and the whole system behaves differently. You tweak a rendering rule and immediately see why the old version felt muddy. You remove a debounce and watch the interface turn feral. Feedback arrives fast enough that your intuition can actually keep up.

I think this is why some of the best engineers and designers have a side habit of building odd little things. Not because every doodle becomes a startup, but because playful work keeps their senses calibrated. It reminds them what software feels like before it becomes a negotiation between six teams and a compliance checklist.

There is also an honesty to toy projects that bigger efforts often lose. In a giant product, weak ideas can survive for a shockingly long time because they are wrapped in process, politics, and sunk cost. In a small experiment, the bad idea usually collapses in public within five minutes. That is healthy. Embarrassing sometimes, sure, but healthy. A toy program will tell you very quickly whether the thing in your head has legs or was just benefiting from PowerPoint lighting.

This is also why I get skeptical when people talk about “just playing around” as if play were the opposite of rigor. Play is often how rigor begins. A scientist fiddles with an apparatus. A musician improvises. A writer drafts a paragraph that will never survive to the final piece. Exploration is not a detour from serious work. It is one of the oldest ways of doing serious work.

Bret Victor has spent years arguing that creators need immediate feedback in order to think well with their materials, and his talk [Inventing on Principle](https://worrydream.com/#!/InventingOnPrinciple) is still one of the clearest cases for that idea. The medium matters, but the broader point is bigger than code demos. If you can see consequences quickly, you can think more honestly. If every experiment is expensive, you become conservative long before you become wise.

Of course, not every toy project is profound. Some are just delightful nonsense, which is also fine. I am pro nonsense. But even nonsense can sharpen taste. You learn what bores you. You learn what patterns recur. You learn which ideas expand when touched and which ones were only funny for twelve seconds.

The best part is that toy programs age well inside your own brain. A failed sketch from six months ago can quietly become the missing piece of a real product later. A visualization you made for fun can become the seed of a dashboard. A micro-tool for one annoying task can become a durable part of your workflow. Serious systems are often assembled from parts that were originally built without the burden of being serious.

So I think we should defend small software more aggressively. Not everything worthwhile needs scale, monetization, or a launch strategy. Sometimes the point of making a thing is to train your eye, test an instinct, or discover that an idea has better bones than you expected.

The toy is not always a prototype for something larger.

Sometimes it is the lab.
