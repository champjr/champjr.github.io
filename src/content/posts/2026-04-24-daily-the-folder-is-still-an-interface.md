---
title: "The Folder Is Still an Interface"
pubDate: 2026-04-24
description: "Why the humble folder keeps surviving every new software wave, including the AI one."
tags: [daily, workflow, files, software, ai]
---

Every few years, software tries to convince us that the folder is about to become obsolete.

Search will replace it. Tags will replace it. Graph views will replace it. Smart workspaces will replace it. AI will replace it, presumably by peering into the glowing chaos of your Downloads folder and declaring itself your new landlord.

And yet the folder persists.

I think that is because folders are not just a storage primitive. They are an interface for thought.

A folder says something very simple and very useful: these things belong together.

That sounds almost insultingly basic, but basic is underrated. A lot of modern software keeps trying to outsmart this idea with systems that are more flexible in theory and more slippery in practice. Search is fantastic when you know what you are looking for. Tags are great when you remember to apply them. AI can be surprisingly good at finding the thing you vaguely described to yourself five minutes ago.

But folders do something different. They create place.

Place matters.

If I have a folder for a writing project, I do not need to remember every file name inside it. I do not need to invent a perfect taxonomy. I just need to remember where that little world lives. Drafts here. Images here. Notes here. Maybe one badly named file that seemed temporary and is now spiritually permanent. That is not failure. That is realism.

This is one reason file systems age so well. They are legible even when everything around them changes. A text editor can disappear. A note-taking app can pivot into an “intelligence layer.” Some startup can decide that exporting your files is no longer aligned with its vision. But a folder full of plain files still makes sense. You can open it on another machine, sync it with boring tools, back it up, zip it, search it, or hand it to your future self without a ritual.

That durability is a feature, not a lack of ambition.

I also think folders encourage a healthier kind of organization than many “smart” systems do. They force you to make a few coarse choices instead of a hundred tiny ones. Good enough beats perfect surprisingly often. Most people do not need a life operating system. They need a place for taxes, a place for photos, a place for receipts, a place for trip planning, and one folder called something like `misc-final-final-actually` that historians will later study.

The folder succeeds because it is forgiving.

This is where some software people get tripped up. They treat manual organization as a burden that should be eliminated entirely. Sometimes that is true. Plenty of software makes people perform pointless filing work. But there is a difference between removing friction and removing agency.

Putting a file somewhere is not always busywork. Sometimes it is the act of deciding what the thing is.

That is especially true in creative work. A project folder becomes a boundary. It gives shape to a mess. It says, this belongs to the same effort, even if the pieces are all different. That is incredibly stabilizing when a project is still half vapor.

I feel this even more now that AI tools are getting woven into everyday workflows. AI is often strongest when it can operate over a clear body of material. A folder is a body of material. It is a natural unit of context. If I point a tool at a project directory, I have already done a useful act of curation. I have said, pay attention to *this* neighborhood.

That is a much better starting point than hoping a global assistant can infer which of my 11 versions of `notes.txt` I meant.

There is a broader lesson here. The future probably does not belong to software that replaces old interfaces just because they are old. It belongs to software that understands why those interfaces survived.

Folders survived because they match how people think about related work. Not always elegantly, not always consistently, but reliably. They are spatial. They are inspectable. They are easy to explain to another person. They degrade gracefully. That last one matters a lot. Good systems should still be understandable on a tired Tuesday.

I am not anti-search, anti-tag, or anti-AI. I use all three constantly. In fact, I think the best modern tools combine them with ordinary files instead of trying to vaporize the file system entirely. Search should help me find the folder. Tags should help me slice across folders. AI should help me understand what is inside them. But the underlying place should still exist.

This is part of why local-first software keeps sounding more attractive. The promise is not just speed or privacy, though both are nice. It is the comforting idea that your work still lives somewhere concrete. A folder you can inspect is a kind of psychological safety. You do not have to trust a product vision statement to believe your files exist.

If you want a good modern example of respecting this idea, [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/) gets a lot of mileage out of the fact that your notes are just files in folders. The app adds power, but it does not pretend the files are beneath it. That is smart.

Maybe that is my actual thesis here: the folder is humble, and humble interfaces tend to last.

They do not demand belief. They do not need a keynote. They just keep holding your stuff in a shape that still makes sense next month.

Honestly, that is more than I can say for half the software industry.
