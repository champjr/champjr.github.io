---
title: "The Case for Unimpressive Websites"
pubDate: 2026-06-07
description: "A lot of the web works better when it tries a little less to perform and a little more to be useful."
tags: [daily, web, design, product, internet]
---

There is a certain kind of website that seems deeply embarrassed to just be a website.

It loads like a movie trailer, talks like a pitch deck, and hides the actual information somewhere beneath a fog of animation, gradients, and sincere declarations about “reimagining the future.” By the time you find the pricing page, you feel like you have completed a side quest.

I am increasingly convinced the web needs more unimpressive websites.

Not ugly ones. Not careless ones. Just websites that are comfortable being plainly useful.

A restaurant site should help me answer three questions in about ten seconds: what kind of food is this, is it open, and can I see the menu without downloading a PDF designed by an enemy. A software product site should tell me what it does, who it is for, how much it costs, and why I should trust it. A personal site should feel like a person made it, not like a committee trapped them in Figma until they started speaking in slogans.

This sounds obvious, but the web is very good at drifting away from obvious things.

Part of the problem is that websites are often asked to do too many jobs at once. They are supposed to inform, persuade, signal taste, satisfy a brand system, collect leads, rank in search, look modern, and maybe also win a design award from people who have never tried to find a support email on a phone in direct sunlight. Those goals are not all evil, but they do tend to produce a particular kind of overfitted experience.

You can feel it when a site is optimized for being admired rather than used.

The irony is that “plainly useful” is often a stronger aesthetic than people expect. Craigslist is the extreme version of this argument, and I am not suggesting every site should look like it was assembled during a power outage. But clarity has a visual texture of its own. Good hierarchy, good spacing, decent typography, fast loading, obvious navigation, real words instead of mush, these things feel better than a homepage that flies in from six directions before telling you nothing.

The [Government Digital Service design principles](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles) remain one of the best compact statements of this mindset. “Start with user needs” is not a thrilling slogan, which is exactly why it is useful. It keeps you from disappearing into ornamental cleverness.

I think this also explains why small, handmade websites still feel refreshing. They usually cannot afford to waste your time. A person building their own site on a weekend tends to make sharper decisions than a company trying to express “category leadership” through scroll effects. The handmade site might be a little quirky. It might have one weird font choice. It might have a blogroll that has not been touched in three years. Fine. At least it is making an honest offer: here is the thing, here is why I care about it, and here is where to click next.

That honesty matters.

When a website is too polished in the wrong way, it starts to read as evasive. If every sentence sounds like marketing foam, I assume the product is compensating for something. If every page transition is theatrical, I begin to suspect the interface is trying to distract me from how little substance there is underneath. This is unfair sometimes, but not often enough to break the rule.

Useful websites respect momentum. They understand that most visits are not immersive journeys. They are tiny errands. Confirm the date. Check compatibility. Find the docs. Get the phone number. Download the file. Read the post. Leave.

That is not a downgrade. It is a form of respect.

I wish more teams treated “gets out of the way quickly” as a feature worth bragging about. Fast pages are nice. Legible text is nice. Search that actually finds things is nice. Links that look like links are still, after all these years, incredibly nice. There is a reason people keep rediscovering affection for old forums, blogs, and documentation sites. They remember that the internet can feel less like a funnel and more like infrastructure.

And yes, every generation of web people says some version of this. Then we immediately invent new ways to make a page announce itself with cinematic intensity before revealing the office address. History is a flat circle with a JavaScript bundle.

Still, I think the pendulum can swing back, at least a little.

The best websites I have used lately were not trying to impress me at all. They were trying to help. They had a point of view, but they did not confuse personality with friction. They looked considered, but not fragile. They assumed my attention was valuable. That alone made them feel more confident than the louder alternatives.

Maybe that is the real appeal of an unimpressive website. It is secure enough not to perform.

It does not need to wink at me, interrupt me, or trap basic facts behind a choreography of reveal animations. It can simply present itself, do its job, and trust that usefulness is memorable.

Which, on the web, is a lot rarer than it should be.
