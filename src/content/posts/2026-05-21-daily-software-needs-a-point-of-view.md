---
title: "Software Needs a Point of View"
pubDate: 2026-05-21
description: "Why the most lovable tools usually stop trying to be everything for everyone."
tags: [software, product, tools, design]
---

A lot of software gets worse in a very predictable way.

It starts out with a sharp idea. You can explain it in one sentence. The defaults make sense. The interface has a personality. Then success arrives, which is nice, but it also brings feature requests, edge cases, enterprise checklists, compatibility promises, and the old familiar temptation to say yes to everything.

That is usually the moment when the software begins to lose its nerve.

I keep coming back to the idea that good tools need a point of view. Not a fake brand voice, not a mascot, not “delight” sprinkled on top like parsley. I mean a real opinion about how work should happen.

A text editor with a point of view says, “Here is the workflow we think is fast.” A note-taking app with a point of view says, “Here is what matters more than infinite customization.” A command-line tool with a point of view says, “We are going to make the common case easy and let the weird case stay slightly weird.”

That last part matters. The common failure mode in product design is not being too opinionated. It is being so afraid of excluding anyone that the product turns into a padded room. Safe, technically accommodating, and strangely exhausting.

There is a reason people still talk about tools like [BBEdit](https://www.barebones.com/products/bbedit/), Obsidian, Raycast, or even old-school Unix utilities with a kind of affectionate respect. You may not agree with every design choice they make, but you can feel that someone made choices on purpose. The product is trying to help you work a certain way, not desperately mirroring every possible preference back at you.

I think this is why “simple” software is often misdiagnosed. People look at a focused tool and say it is missing things. Sometimes that is true. But sometimes the missing thing is actually the product defending its shape.

There is a big difference between a missing capability and a refused distraction.

One of my favorite examples is defaults. Great software often has defaults that are just opinionated enough to save you from your own tendency to over-tune. It picks sensible spacing, sane filenames, readable typography, decent keyboard shortcuts, a backup strategy, or one obvious path through the interface. It does not open by asking you to define seventeen preferences so it can avoid the responsibility of making a choice.

People say they want flexibility, and they do, right up until flexibility becomes a second job.

This is where product teams get trapped. Every new option feels merciful in isolation. A toggle for this. An override for that. A legacy mode, an advanced mode, a compatibility layer, a hidden setting that turns out not to stay hidden for long. Eventually the software starts to feel less like a tool and more like a treaty negotiation between all historical versions of itself.

That kind of accumulation is hard to reverse because each addition has a defender. Someone uses it. Someone needs it. Someone sold it. Someone filed a ticket three years ago and still feels strongly. So the product grows not according to a theory of usefulness, but according to sediment.

The trouble is that sediment has a user experience.

A strong point of view does not mean being stubborn for sport. It means knowing what the product is for, who it helps most, and which compromises actually break the spell. Sometimes the right answer is no. Sometimes the right answer is not yet. Sometimes the right answer is, honestly, you may want a different tool.

That last answer is underrated. Not every piece of software should try to become the platform layer for your entire life. Some things should remain gloriously specific. A writing app can just help you write. A launcher can just help you get around your computer faster. A static site generator can just turn files into a website without inviting you into a new theology.

The funniest thing is that opinionated software often creates more loyalty, not less. Users do not merely adapt to it, they build taste around it. They learn the shortcuts. They absorb the patterns. They begin to understand not just how the tool works, but what it believes.

That is a stronger bond than generic convenience.

It also makes software easier to improve. When a team knows its point of view, decisions get faster. New features can be judged against a standard that is deeper than “will somebody ask for this.” Documentation gets clearer. Onboarding gets easier. The product begins to feel coherent instead of merely complete.

I do not think every app should be rigid. Flexibility has its place, especially in infrastructure, accessibility, and tools that must fit many environments. But even there, the best systems usually have an opinion about the happy path. They do not dump infinite possibility in your lap and call it empowerment.

Maybe that is the real test. When I open a tool, does it feel like it wants to help me do something well, or does it feel like it wants to avoid being blamed?

Those are very different energies.

The software I end up loving usually has a little courage in it. It is willing to privilege a workflow. It is willing to make a default stick. It is willing to leave some things out. It trusts that clarity is kinder than endless accommodation.

More products should try that. Fewer should aspire to become a Swiss Army knife made entirely of settings panels.
