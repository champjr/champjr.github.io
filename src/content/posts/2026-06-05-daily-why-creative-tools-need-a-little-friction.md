---
title: "Why Creative Tools Need a Little Friction"
pubDate: 2026-06-05
description: "The best creative tools do not remove every bump in the road. Sometimes a little resistance is what helps ideas take shape."
tags: [daily, creativity, tools, software, workflow]
---

Software people love the phrase “reduce friction.”

Usually for good reason. If I am trying to pay a bill, reset a password, or file an expense report, I want the software to feel like a well-oiled grocery cart, not one with a wheel that keeps trying to join a different store.

But I think creative tools are a little different.

In creative work, removing all friction can make the work worse.

That sounds backwards, because software has spent the last decade treating any pause, constraint, or moment of deliberation as a product bug. Everything should be instant. Everything should autosave, autocomplete, auto-suggest, auto-organize, auto-summarize, and possibly auto-congratulate itself.

I like convenience as much as anyone. I am not campaigning for software that behaves like a Victorian lockbox. But I do think there is a meaningful difference between useful smoothness and total slipperiness.

Some tools are better when they push back a little.

A blank note in a writing app is good frictionless design. Fighting a sync conflict at midnight is not noble suffering. But a tool that asks you to choose a title, name a version, trim a draft, pick a color palette, or commit to a structure is not necessarily getting in your way. Sometimes it is helping you think.

Good friction is often disguised as form.

This is why so many people still like index cards, notebooks, plain text files, or simple drawing tools even when richer, smarter options are available. Those older or smaller tools do less for you, which sometimes means they leave more room for your own judgment. They do not try to finish the sentence before you understand what you mean.

I think that matters more now because modern software is getting very good at producing plausible momentum. It can make you feel productive before you have actually made a decision.

An AI writing tool can turn a rough idea into a polished paragraph in seconds. That is impressive. It is also a little dangerous. The speed can blur an important distinction: did the tool help you express a thought, or did it help you skip having one?

That is not an anti-AI point, exactly. I think these systems are genuinely useful. I use them. But they work best when they support attention instead of replacing it. If every rough edge disappears, you can slide from curiosity into mush without noticing.

The composer Brian Eno has talked for years about the value of constraints in creative work, and his *Oblique Strategies* deck is basically a catalog of productive resistance, little prompts that interrupt habitual thinking and force a new angle: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_Strategies>.

That idea shows up everywhere.

A camera with only one focal length changes how you look.

A text editor without endless formatting options changes how you draft.

A music tool with a weird limitation often becomes identifiable by the kind of songs it encourages.

Even programming languages have this quality. Some languages feel like they are trying to prevent you from making certain classes of mistakes. Others feel like they handed you a chainsaw and said, with warm optimism, “be free.” Both approaches have their place. But limitations are not always enemies. Sometimes they are style engines.

This is also why I am skeptical of products that brag about being able to do everything in one place. It sounds efficient. It usually means the tool has the personality of a hotel conference room.

The more a tool tries to become universal, the more likely it is to smooth away the very edges that make certain kinds of work vivid. Specialized tools can be annoying, sure. They can also be memorable. They invite technique. They create preference. They let people develop taste.

And taste, inconveniently, does not emerge from pure automation.

Taste is built in tiny acts of selection. This word instead of that one. This crop instead of that crop. This silence instead of another line of explanation. Those choices often happen in moments where the tool does not instantly resolve the tension for you.

That tension is not waste. It is part of the craft.

I think the healthiest product question is not “how do we remove every bit of friction?” It is “which friction is accidental, and which friction is actually doing useful cognitive work?”

That is a harder question, because it requires knowing what the tool is for.

If the job is coordination, payment, scheduling, or retrieval, yes, sand down the edges. Nobody needs a spiritually enriching password reset flow.

But if the job is writing, sketching, composing, editing, brainstorming, prototyping, or exploring half-formed ideas, then a little resistance can be a feature. Not a lot. Not enough to make the tool precious or annoying. Just enough to keep the user present.

A good creative tool should feel like a collaborator with manners.

It should help you move.

It should not do all the moving for you.

The best ones create an odd balance of ease and insistence. They remove the boring obstacles, then leave the meaningful decisions in your hands. They know the difference between helping and hovering.

That balance is hard to get right, and I think product teams miss it because “friction” sounds universally bad in a slide deck. Nobody wants to stand up in a planning meeting and say, “what if we made this slightly less convenient on purpose?” That sentence tends to end careers or at least postpone promotion cycles.

Still, I think it is true.

Some software should be frictionless.

Some software should be friction-aware.

And creative tools, at their best, are not polished ice rinks. They are good shoes on interesting ground.
