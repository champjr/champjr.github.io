Design tools have a habit of becoming entire planets. Penpot is interesting because it gives you a modern, collaborative UI design and prototyping workflow, but does it in the open.

Penpot is an open-source design platform for interfaces, product mockups, design systems, and clickable prototypes. If you have used tools like Figma, Sketch, or Adobe XD, the basic idea will feel familiar: draw screens, build reusable components, hand designs to developers, and keep everyone arguing about button spacing in one place. The difference is that Penpot is open source, self-hostable, and built with web standards in mind.

## Quick tour

At a glance, Penpot is a browser-based workspace for designing apps, websites, and UI systems. You can create artboards, draw vector shapes, add text, make reusable components, define styles, and connect screens into prototypes. In other words, it covers the practical day-to-day stuff teams actually need, not just the shiny demo stuff.

A few features make it stand out:

- **Real collaboration.** Multiple people can work in the same project, comment on designs, and keep feedback attached to the work instead of scattered across twelve tabs and one cursed group chat.
- **Design systems and reusable components.** Penpot is very good at turning one-off mockups into something repeatable, which is where a lot of design workflows either mature or descend into chaos.
- **SVG and web-friendly foundations.** Because it leans on open web standards, it feels closer to the actual medium many teams are designing for. That is a nice change from tools that sometimes feel like they were invented on a different planet than the browser.
- **Self-hosting option.** If your team cares about ownership, compliance, or simply not waking up to a surprise pricing plot twist, this is a big deal.
- **Developer handoff that does not feel like punishment.** Devs can inspect styles, spacing, colors, and assets without needing a ceremonial screen-share just to learn what `16px` means today.

## Why it’s cool

Penpot is cool because it is tackling a category that usually gets treated as untouchable. Design collaboration has been dominated by proprietary tools for years, and a lot of teams quietly accept that as the natural order of things. Penpot basically says, “what if this part of the stack could be open too?” I like that energy.

It is also cool because it is not trying to win on ideology alone. “Open source” is nice, but if the product is clunky, people bounce. Penpot is compelling because it is genuinely usable. It feels like a serious attempt to give product teams a practical, modern workflow rather than a noble but painful alternative.

There is also a bigger idea here. Design systems, UI specs, and product prototypes shape how software gets built. Keeping those artifacts in a tool you can inspect, host, and extend yourself is appealing. It gives teams more control over a very central part of product development. That is not just philosophically nice, it is operationally nice.

## Who it’s for

Penpot is especially worth a look for:

- product teams that want a collaborative UI design tool without full vendor lock-in
- startups watching software spend and trying to keep the stack flexible
- agencies and freelancers who want an open-source-friendly workflow
- developers who appreciate design files that map more naturally to the web
- organizations that need self-hosting for security, compliance, or governance reasons

If your team is deeply embedded in another design ecosystem, a full migration might take some patience. But for new projects, open-source teams, or anyone design-tool-curious, Penpot is a very credible option.

## Getting started

The smallest possible first step is honestly the easiest one: open Penpot's hosted version in your browser and create a throwaway design.

Go to the official site, sign up, make a new project, draw a couple of frames, turn one element into a component, and connect two screens with a prototype interaction. You will know pretty quickly whether it clicks for you.

If you want to get more hands-on, Penpot also provides self-hosting docs so you can run your own instance later. But for a first date, the browser version is enough.
