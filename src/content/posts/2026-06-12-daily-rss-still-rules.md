---
title: "RSS Is Still One of the Healthiest Things on the Internet"
pubDate: 2026-06-12
description: "An old, unglamorous standard still does a better job of delivering the web than many modern feeds built to negotiate for your attention."
tags: [daily, internet, rss, web, workflow]
---

RSS is one of those technologies people keep accidentally eulogizing.

Every few years somebody declares it dead, or niche, or hopelessly old-fashioned, usually with the tone people use when talking about a once-beloved mall. Sad to see it go. Anyway, here is a new app that uses machine learning to summarize posts you never asked it to select for you.

And yet RSS keeps hanging around.

I think that is because RSS solves a very real problem in a very clean way. It answers a simple question: how do I keep up with things I care about on the web without asking a giant platform to mediate the relationship?

The answer, delightfully, is still just: subscribe to a feed.

That sounds almost suspiciously modest in 2026. We live in an era when a lot of software wants to be your personalized operating system for attention. Feeds are ranked. Notifications are optimized. Discovery is gamified. Everything wants to be a little bit sticky and a little bit persuasive.

RSS, by contrast, is refreshingly indifferent.

It does not care about your engagement strategy. It does not throw in suggested posts from strangers because you lingered on a headline about mechanical keyboards. It does not quietly turn “show me updates from these sites” into “show me whatever keeps me scrolling.”

That restraint is not a bug. It is the whole appeal.

A good RSS reader feels like plumbing, not a casino.

I mean that as praise.

One of the odd things about the modern web is that abundance is not actually the hard part anymore. There is more good stuff to read than any person could finish in ten lifetimes. The hard part is preserving intent. If I say I want updates from a handful of blogs, newsletters, comics, research labs, or niche hobby sites, I want exactly that. Not a vibes-based approximation. Not an “enhanced” version. Just the thing.

RSS is good at intent.

It also makes the web feel bigger in a healthy way. Platform feeds tend to flatten everything into the same format and the same incentive structure. Every post starts to feel like it was shaped by the same invisible product manager asking whether it can be a bit more engaging.

Feeds pull in a different world. Personal blogs next to major publications. weird little changelogs next to thoughtful essays. a one-person site about transit maps next to release notes from a developer tool. It reminds you that the internet is not one giant app. It is a messy federation of people publishing things for their own reasons.

That is a much nicer internet.

There is also a practical benefit I do not think gets enough respect: RSS is calmer.

When I open an RSS reader, I am usually in a collecting mindset. I am surveying. Skimming. Saving a few things for later. The interaction has edges. I know what I am there to do, and I know when I am done.

That is not true of many modern feeds, which are designed to turn a quick check into an accidental quarter hour. You go in for one update and come out holding eight opinions you did not have before, three fragments of discourse, and a vague sense that everyone online has become emotionally overclocked.

RSS has many virtues, but one of its best is that it is a lousy vehicle for ambient hysteria.

Of course, RSS is not perfect. Some sites do not publish feeds. Some feeds are truncated or messy. Discovery can be harder if you are used to platforms spoon-feeding everything. There is setup involved, which already places it outside the modern growth playbook. Asking people to choose sources on purpose is, apparently, considered a hostile onboarding flow.

Still, the old standard keeps proving that a lot of users do not actually need more algorithmic curation. They need better control.

That is part of why tools like [Feedbin](https://feedbin.com/) and other readers keep finding loyal audiences. They offer a simple bargain: bring your own taste, and we will help you keep it organized.

There is something almost rebellious about software that refuses to decide what you should care about.

I like that RSS is boring infrastructure. I like that the core idea has been stable for ages. I like that the [RSS 2.0 spec](https://cyber.harvard.edu/rss/rss.html) reads like a reminder that not every durable internet protocol needs to arrive with a grand theory of world domination.

Mostly, I like what RSS protects.

It protects direct relationships between publishers and readers. It protects the possibility of following people outside the largest platforms. It protects a version of the web where “keeping up” does not automatically mean surrendering your attention to ranking systems designed around business goals you did not choose.

That does not mean every person should become a full-time feed enthusiast with a lovingly curated taxonomy of folders. Although, to be clear, that life is available if you want it, and it has a certain charm.

It just means we should notice how rare it is for a piece of internet infrastructure to remain both useful and respectful.

RSS still feels respectful.

It gives you the updates. It gets out of the way. It assumes you are capable of deciding what is worth reading.

More software should have that kind of confidence in people.
