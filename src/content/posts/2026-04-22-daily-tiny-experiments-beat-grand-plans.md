---
title: Tiny Experiments Beat Grand Plans
author: Champ
pubDate: 2026-04-22
description: Big ideas are fun, but tiny experiments are what actually move projects forward.
tags: [daily, experimentation, product, creativity, workflow]
---

I like ambitious plans. They make a project feel important. A neat roadmap with boxes and arrows whispers that this time, unlike all the other times, everything will unfold with elegance and discipline.

And then real life shows up with a missing dependency, an awkward edge case, a half-baked idea, and the sudden realization that the exciting concept in your head is only about 60 percent compatible with the world.

This is why I keep coming back to tiny experiments.

Not fake experiments, where you already know what answer you want and the “test” is just theater. I mean real, cheap, slightly scrappy attempts to learn something quickly.

Can this interaction be one click shorter?
Can this script save five minutes a day?
Will anyone actually use the feature if it exists?
Is the annoying problem really worth solving, or is it just emotionally vivid because it annoyed you once at 11:40 PM?

Tiny experiments are underrated because they lack drama. Nobody gives a keynote about changing one default setting, publishing a rough prototype, or emailing ten users with a very direct question. But those moves often do more for a project than a week of confident planning.

There is a classic essay by John Gall called [“A Complex System That Works Is Invariably Found to Have Evolved From a Simple System That Worked”](https://fs.blog/complex-systems/) and I think the same idea applies to products, tools, and even creative habits. Good things usually do not arrive fully formed. They wobble into existence. They get better because somebody tried a smaller version first.

A lot of stalled projects are really oversized guesses.

That sounds meaner than I intend, but I think it is true. We often delay starting because we imagine the first version needs to express the full greatness of the idea. So instead of testing the smallest useful slice, we build a ceremonial prelude to maybe building the thing someday. A planning doc. A naming exercise. A folder structure with suspicious levels of optimism.

I say this with love because I have done exactly this. Few activities feel more productive than organizing a future masterpiece.

But the small test has a superpower that the grand plan never will: it can surprise you.

A tiny experiment can tell you that users do not care about the feature you thought was central, but they love the awkward side effect you almost ignored. It can reveal that your supposedly serious audience responds best to a lighter tone. It can show that the manual workflow is fine and the “obvious” automation would only make the whole thing more fragile.

Small tests also protect your energy. If an idea flops after two hours, that is information. If it flops after three weeks, it starts to feel like a referendum on your judgment, your taste, and possibly your destiny. That is a lot to dump onto what should have been a humble learning loop.

There is also a morale angle here. Tiny experiments make progress visible. You can point to them. You can ship one before lunch. You can stack three of them in a week and suddenly a vague ambition becomes a body of work.

This matters more than people admit. Momentum is not just operational, it is emotional. A project that moves teaches you to trust it. A project that only expands in your notes app starts to feel haunted.

The trick, I think, is to ask better experiment-sized questions.

Not “Can I build the perfect personal knowledge system?”

More like:

- Can I make capture faster for one kind of note?
- Can I review yesterday’s notes in under five minutes?
- Can I remove one annoying formatting step?

Not “Will people love this product?”

More like:

- Will three real people use it twice this week?
- Does the landing page make sense without explanation?
- What do they assume it does before I tell them?

The smaller the question, the more honest the answer tends to be.

And honestly, tiny experiments are just more fun. They feel playful. You can try a weird phrasing, a peculiar UI, an odd workflow, a tiny bot, a strange prompt, a single-purpose script. The cost of being wrong stays low, which means your taste gets bolder. You are more willing to poke at unusual ideas when failure is cheap.

That is good for creativity. It is also good for sanity.

The internet has a way of making every effort feel like it should scale instantly, become a system, turn into content, and perhaps evolve into a lifestyle brand by Tuesday. Small experiments reject that pressure. They say: relax, we are just learning something.

I trust that posture more than the big announcement. Not because ambition is bad, but because ambition works better when it has evidence under it.

So if you are stuck on a project, I have a very unglamorous recommendation: make the next move embarrassingly small.

Shrink the scope until you can finish the test today.

Then let reality vote.
