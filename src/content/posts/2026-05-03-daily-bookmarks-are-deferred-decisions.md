I have a theory about bookmarks.

Most of them are not really bookmarks.

They are deferred decisions.

When we save a link, we like to imagine we are building a useful personal library. A thoughtful archive. A future treasure chest of articles, tools, recipes, essays, repos, and weird corners of the internet we will definitely revisit when we become the kind of organized, intellectually consistent person our browser seems to believe in.

In reality, a lot of bookmarking is just polite procrastination.

We are telling ourselves, “this seems important enough that I do not want to lose it, but not important enough that I want to decide what to do with it right now.” So the link goes into bookmarks, or read-later, or a notes app, or a tab group that has silently become a retirement community.

I do this all the time, which is how I know the pattern is real.

The internet has made this habit extremely easy. Saving information is now so frictionless that the act can masquerade as thinking. One click, maybe two, and suddenly we feel marginally more prepared, more informed, more in possession of the future. It is the productivity equivalent of buying vegetables and feeling healthier before you have eaten them.

To be clear, I am not anti-bookmark. I love good collections. I also think read-later tools can be genuinely useful. [Pocket](https://getpocket.com/) did a lot to popularize the idea that the web needed a pause button, and that instinct was right even if many of us turned the pause button into a second inbox.

The problem is not saving things. The problem is pretending that saving and deciding are the same action.

They are not.

A real decision sounds more like this:

- I am going to read this today.
- I am saving this because I want to reference it for a project.
- I do not need this, but I am anxious about losing it.
- This is interesting, but not interesting enough.

That last category is where a lot of digital clutter is born.

There is something emotionally sneaky about links. They are small. They take up almost no visible space. A hundred unread essays do not look as intimidating as a hundred unread books stacked in your hallway, even though the psychological debt is remarkably similar. The web lets us accumulate intentions at industrial scale.

And because the cost of saving is tiny, we often skip the useful question: what exactly am I preserving here?

Sometimes the honest answer is the information itself. Great. Save it.

Sometimes it is a reminder of a mood. An ambition. A version of yourself that wants to learn analog synthesizers, comparative urban policy, or how to make a proper cacio e pepe without accidentally creating pasta mortar.

Sometimes it is social. You save a link because someone smart sent it, and deleting it feels a bit like disagreeing with them.

Sometimes it is guilt in URL form.

I think a better bookmarking habit is not “save less” so much as “decide slightly earlier.” Not with a grand taxonomy project, because those often become another form of delay. Just with a little more honesty at capture time.

If I were designing the ideal bookmarking tool, it would ask one mildly annoying but useful question every time: *why are you saving this?*

Not enough to slow you down dramatically. Just enough to force a classification of intent.

Reference.
Read later.
Steal this idea later.
Maybe buy this.
This made me laugh.
This is for a specific person.
I am saving this because I do not trust my future self to find it again.

That last one might actually be the default setting for the modern web.

Search is better than it used to be, but discoverability is still fragile. Pages disappear. Titles are vague. Sites redesign themselves into oblivion. A link you could find instantly today becomes ungoogleable six months later because the original headline was something like “Thoughts on Building Better Systems” and apparently half the internet has had thoughts on building better systems.

So yes, there is a practical reason to save aggressively.

But I still think we should admit that many saved links are not knowledge management. They are negotiation management. We are negotiating with our own attention.

“Not now, but maybe later.”

That sentence runs half of modern computing.

It is in bookmarks, open tabs, notifications, drafts, wish lists, carts, watch queues, and desktop files named `final-final-actually-use-this-one`. A lot of digital life is just structured deferral.

Which is fine, up to a point. Deferral is not failure. Sometimes it is wisdom. You do not need to process every interesting thing on contact. Curiosity without immediate obligation is one of the best things about the internet.

But deferred decisions do eventually harden into ambient stress if you never clear them.

That is why I think the healthiest bookmark collections are not the biggest ones. They are the ones with a visible relationship to actual use. A smaller pile that feeds projects, conversations, writing, and real moments of learning is worth more than a giant museum of good intentions.

So lately I have been trying a simple rule.

When I save something, I try to decide which of three futures it belongs to:

Read soon. Use later. Or release.

If it is “read soon,” it goes somewhere temporary.

If it is “use later,” it earns a better home, maybe with a note about why it matters.

If it is neither, I try to be brave enough to let it go.

This does not make me a perfectly disciplined internet monk. I still have enough saved links to accidentally construct a minor liberal arts curriculum. But it does make the pile feel more honest.

And honestly, that may be the best we can ask from our tools.

Not perfect order. Just fewer lies.
