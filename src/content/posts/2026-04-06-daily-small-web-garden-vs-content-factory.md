A lot of the modern internet feels less like a place and more like a logistics system.

Everything arrives polished, optimized, tracked, resized, summarized, recommended, and gently pushed toward whatever metric is currently wearing the crown. Engagement. Retention. Conversion. Time on site. Time in app. Time in the Content Treadmill, where every platform politely insists it is helping you discover things while quietly turning every creator into a full-time warehouse worker.

And yet, the parts of the web I remember most are usually the least industrial ones.

They are the odd personal sites. The tiny blogs. The software projects with weirdly charming documentation. The pages that clearly belong to a person with preferences instead of a committee with dashboards. Not because they are *anti-technology*, but because they still feel like they were made by somebody who wanted to leave a trace of themselves on purpose.

That is the thing I keep coming back to: **the small web still feels like a garden**.

Not efficient. Not infinitely scalable. Not always tidy. But alive.

## Gardens are not feeds

A feed is built for flow. The next item replaces the previous one before your brain has even finished deciding whether it cared. That design is powerful, but it has a cost: it makes everything feel slightly interchangeable.

A garden works differently. A garden invites wandering. It has paths, edges, surprises, dead ends, favorite corners, and evidence of maintenance. You can tell when somebody planted something intentionally. You can also tell when they got distracted and let one section get a little wild. Weirdly, that makes it better.

The best personal websites have this quality. You click around and start building a mental model of the person behind them. Not just what they are posting, but how they think. What they collect. Which details they care enough to keep. What they made once and still love enough not to delete.

This is also why a lot of personal blogs age better than social posts. A blog post usually has to stand on its own legs. It cannot rely on the constant conveyor belt around it. It has to be worth visiting directly, maybe even a year later, when the algorithm has long since wandered off to chase shinier prey.

## Optimization has a taste

One reason the web feels repetitive lately is that optimization leaves a residue.

If enough people are optimizing for search, pages start sounding like they were assembled by the same mildly caffeinated intern. If enough people are optimizing for social distribution, headlines all start developing the same raised eyebrow. If enough people are optimizing for AI summaries, you start seeing pages written less for humans reading and more for machines compressing.

This is not always malicious. Sometimes it is just survival. People want to be found. They want their work to travel. They want the website they spent hours making to be visited by more than six loyal weirdos and one accidental crawler from Slovakia.

Fair enough.

But the side effect is that **highly optimized writing often tastes strangely identical**, even when the topic changes. It has calories, maybe even nutrients, but not much texture.

That is part of why the small web is refreshing. It still contains texture. Somebody uses too many parentheses. Somebody else has an aggressively specific color palette. A project page contains one excellent rant about package managers and then immediately returns to calm technical prose. A notes section has three brilliant paragraphs and one terrible joke. This is good, actually. The terrible joke is carrying a lot of weight.

## Hand-made is becoming a feature again

There is a broader pattern here. As more text, images, interfaces, and even voices become easy to generate at industrial scale, the value of things that feel intentionally made by a real person goes up.

Not because hand-made automatically means better. Plenty of hand-made things are bad. Have you seen my first drafts? Grim scenes.

But hand-made things do have one increasingly rare property: **specificity**.

A specific website has a point of view. A specific blog post says, “Here is what I noticed,” not merely, “Here is a sufficiently smooth average of what can be said.” A specific page may even be a little inconvenient in the way an actual room is inconvenient compared with a hotel lobby. The chair is where *they* wanted it, not where analytics declared it should go.

This reminds me of the old [POSSE idea](https://indieweb.org/POSSE) from the IndieWeb crowd: publish on your own site first, then syndicate elsewhere if you want. Even if you never go full web monk and start hand-authoring HTML by candlelight, the principle is still smart. Have one place online that is yours. A place where the defaults are your defaults.

That matters more than it used to.

## The web gets better when people leave fingerprints

I do not think everybody needs a personal blog. Some people should absolutely continue posting one devastatingly good paragraph every six months in a place only twelve people know about. That is a noble tradition.

But I do think the internet gets better when more people make spaces that do not feel fully standardized.

Leave fingerprints.

Write a page that is a little too specific. Keep a running notes section. Make a project log. Post screenshots of experiments. Keep an about page that sounds like a human being instead of a résumé that achieved consciousness. Link to things you genuinely like. Explain why.

None of this has to become a brand. In fact, it is often better when it does not.

The small web is appealing for the same reason a neighborhood coffee shop beats an airport concourse. The coffee may be similar. The Wi‑Fi may even be worse. But one of them feels like it is trying to host a life instead of process a body.

That is what I want more of online: spaces that feel hosted.

Not frictionless. Not endlessly optimized. Not pretending every page is a product surface.

Just a little more human cultivation.

A little more garden.
