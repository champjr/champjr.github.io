---
title: "Bookmarks Are Still a Superpower"
pubDate: 2026-04-19
description: "A small defense of one of the oldest, least glamorous, and most useful tools on the web."
tags: [daily, web, workflow, internet, tools]
---

There is a category of software that gets less respect than it deserves because it is old, plain, and not especially marketable. Bookmarks live in that category.

Nobody launches a startup and says, with a straight face, “We’re reinventing bookmarks.” At best, they call it curation, knowledge capture, discovery, or personal search. Then they wrap it in a sleek interface, add an AI summary button, and charge you a monthly fee that somehow costs more than a decent lunch.

Meanwhile, the humble bookmark is still sitting there in your browser like a wrench in a drawer, quietly being useful.

I think bookmarks are underrated, mostly because we keep asking them to be more glamorous than they need to be. A bookmark is not a second brain. It is not a PKM system. It is not your autobiography. It is just a way to say: I will want to find this again.

That sentence alone is more powerful than it sounds.

The modern web is optimized for immediacy. Everything wants to be consumed now, reacted to now, forwarded now, forgotten now. Even good links are treated like produce with a short shelf life. If you do not read them immediately, they fall into the void between open tabs, chat history, and that one note app you swore you were finally going to organize.

A bookmark pushes back on that a little. It says this page has future value, not just present value.

That matters.

A well-kept bookmark collection becomes a personal index of the internet. Not the entire internet, obviously. More like your internet, which is the only version anyone ever really uses. It reflects what you return to, what you trust, what you find beautiful, and what you keep needing when your memory fails five minutes before a deadline.

Some of my favorite categories are the least impressive on paper:

- docs pages I always forget the URL for
- articles that explain a concept unusually well
- weird tools I do not need yet, but absolutely will someday
- reference pages that save me from low-grade re-Googling
- tiny websites that feel like they were made by a person instead of a committee

That last category is especially important. The web is still full of small, excellent corners, but they are easy to lose. Search is worse than it used to feel. Social feeds are bad at resurfacing old value. And recommendation systems are very good at giving you more of what is popular, which is not the same thing as what is good.

Bookmarks let you keep your own taste in circulation.

I also think bookmarks are one of the healthiest forms of digital optimism. They assume continuity. They assume there will be a later. Later, you will build that thing. Later, you will fix that bug. Later, you will read the essay. Later, you will remember why this link mattered.

Sometimes later never comes, of course. That is fine. A bookmark archive is allowed to contain ambition, not just execution. If anything, that makes it more human.

The trick is not to turn bookmarks into a guilt pile. This is where a lot of people give up. They bookmark as if they are creating a syllabus for a future version of themselves who has perfect discipline, unlimited time, and an ergonomic chair blessed by the productivity gods.

That person does not exist.

A better approach is to treat bookmarks like shelves, not obligations. Save fewer things. Name folders in plain English. Make peace with a little mess. Revisit occasionally. Delete aggressively when something turns out not to matter.

If you want one genuinely useful habit, it is this: keep a short “repeat visit” folder for pages you actually reopen, not pages you merely admire in theory. That folder becomes a map of your real work. Documentation, dashboards, reference pages, style guides, datasets, source material, forums with one oddly perfect answer from 2017, all of it. It is a practical record of where your attention genuinely goes.

Browsers have gotten better at search inside bookmarks too, which makes the whole system more forgiving. You do not need a taxonomist’s soul anymore. You just need enough structure that future-you can recover the trail. Good enough is enough.

There is also something satisfying about bookmarks because they are boring in the right way. They do not try to become a lifestyle. They do not ask for daily streaks. They do not send notifications that sound like a lab rat casino. They just wait.

I love software that knows how to wait.

Maybe that is the broader point. Some tools are valuable precisely because they do not compete for the center of your attention. They sit off to the side, accumulating usefulness. Calendars do this at their best. Good file systems do this. Plain text does this. Bookmarks, when you stop expecting them to be magical, do this too.

The web’s official memory is increasingly unstable. Pages disappear, search results drift, platforms rise and sink into mud. If you care about your own continuity online, even a little, you should keep some kind of personal trail. Browser bookmarks are the simplest version of that idea, and simple is often underrated until the moment you need it.

If you want inspiration, the [Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/) is a reminder that preservation is one of the web’s most noble activities. On a much smaller scale, a bookmark is a private version of the same instinct. Not preservation for everyone, just preservation for you.

And honestly, that is enough.

You do not need a grand system. You probably just need a folder called “useful stuff” and the discipline to admit that this, too, is infrastructure.
