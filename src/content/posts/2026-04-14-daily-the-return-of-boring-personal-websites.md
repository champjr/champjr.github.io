---
title: "The Return of Boring Personal Websites"
pubDate: 2026-04-14
description: "Why the most refreshing thing on the modern internet might be a plain personal site with a pulse." 
tags: [web, writing, internet, blogging]
---

A lot of the modern internet feels like it was designed by growth teams trapped in an escape room.

Everything slides, nudges, subscribes, expands, auto-plays, re-targets, summarizes, and gently insists that you are one click away from a “game-changing” newsletter written in the voice of a caffeinated brand strategist.

Against that backdrop, a boring personal website feels almost rebellious.

I mean *boring* as a compliment.

Not dead. Not lazy. Not ugly on purpose in a way that still somehow takes 14 MB to load. I mean a site that does a few normal things well. A homepage. Some writing. Maybe a links page. Maybe a list of projects. Maybe a weird little note about what the author is tinkering with this month. No conversion funnel. No “content engine.” No popup asking whether you want updates before the page has earned the right to say hello.

I think these sites are getting more appealing precisely because the rest of the web has become so optimized.

For years, the internet kept rewarding systems that turned expression into throughput. Publish more. Package harder. Find your niche. Build a pipeline. Repurpose across formats. Clip the clip from the clip. Somewhere along the way, a lot of websites stopped feeling like places and started feeling like vending machines for personal brand output.

That is efficient, sure. It is also a little bleak.

A good personal site still feels like visiting a place made by a person.

That difference matters.

When a site is small and clearly owned, you can feel the author’s taste in the structure. What gets pinned. What gets hidden. Whether they care about archives. Whether they write in bursts or maintain a steady rhythm. Whether their links page is a careful garden or a glorious pile of internet raccoon treasures. These are tiny signals, but they add up to something the big platforms are very bad at preserving: **shape**.

Platforms flatten people into feeds. Personal websites let them have edges.

Part of this is technical. The web is one of the few places where you can still publish something durable without asking a platform for permission every six months. If you control your own domain and files, your work is less exposed to algorithm changes, product shutdowns, or the managerial whim that decides your writing should now live behind a reel tab.

That is one reason the old IndieWeb idea still holds up. The movement’s principle of [owning your domain and publishing on the open web](https://indieweb.org/why) is not nostalgia. It is practical internet hygiene. If you care about writing, notes, research, or just having a corner of the web that is *yours*, that architecture buys you a lot.

It also buys you a healthier relationship with attention.

On a platform, the temptation is to think in performance terms. How did this do? Should I post at a different time? Is this format favored right now? Did the machine smile upon my little offering? The whole environment quietly teaches you to treat your own thoughts like inventory.

On a personal site, especially a small one, that pressure drops.

Not completely, obviously. People can still obsess over analytics on their own domain like tiny Victorian accountants. But the default posture changes. You are more likely to ask, “Is this worth keeping?” than “Will this travel?” That is a much saner editorial question.

I also think boring personal sites are good for readers.

The current web is full of pages that are technically visible and functionally unreadable. They are padded with SEO throat-clearing, interrupted by ad slots, and arranged so that the actual useful sentence appears only after the page has completed several small ceremonies. You came for an idea and got an obstacle course.

A simple personal site often skips all of that. It lets the writing be the event.

That can feel almost suspiciously pleasant now, like finding a diner that still serves coffee in a thick mug and does not call fries a “crispy potato experience.” You keep waiting for the catch. Then you realize there is no catch. The page just loads.

Beautiful stuff.

There is also a social reason I like these sites. They make the internet feel more plural.

A web made mostly of giant platforms has a sameness problem. Different people, same templates. Different voices, same boxes. Different communities, same engagement mechanics. Personal websites break that monoculture a little. They remind you that the internet can still be composed of many small rooms instead of five loud malls.

And yes, not every personal site is great. Some are abandoned. Some are cryptic in a way that is less “intriguing” and more “the navigation appears to have been designed by a sleep-deprived wizard.” Some use type that seems chosen specifically to punish the eyeballs. Freedom includes the freedom to make baffling design decisions. That is fine. I would still rather browse a weird human website than a perfectly optimized content slab.

The good news is that making a personal site is much easier now than it used to be. Static site generators are decent. Hosting is cheap. Plain Markdown remains one of the internet’s best anti-nonsense technologies. You do not need a grand strategy. You just need a page and the willingness to put something on it.

Maybe that is the real appeal.

A boring personal website is one of the last places online where you can be present without turning yourself into a product.

You can publish a note, an essay, a reading list, a sketch, a changelog of your current obsessions, whatever. It does not need to scale. It does not need to dominate a feed. It just needs to exist, reliably, in public, with your name on it.

That feels increasingly rare.

And maybe, if the internet is lucky, increasingly contagious.
