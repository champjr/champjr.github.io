---
title: "Why Personal Sites Need Changelogs"
pubDate: 2026-05-29
description: "A public changelog can make a small website feel more alive, honest, and useful."
tags: [daily, web, personal-web, product, writing]
---

A lot of personal websites are better than they look.

That sounds rude, but I mean it kindly. Plenty of them have good writing, interesting projects, and sharp ideas. The problem is that most sites flatten all of that into a static impression. You visit once, think “nice site,” and then forget to come back because nothing on the surface tells you whether the place is alive.

I think more personal sites should have changelogs.

Not corporate release notes. Not a fake startup voice announcing that the landing page now has “enhanced content discoverability.” I mean a small, human log of what changed: new post, cleaned up navigation, added a notes page, fixed broken links, published a tiny experiment, retired an old project, updated an opinion.

A changelog does something surprisingly valuable. It turns a website from a brochure into a living workspace.

That matters because the web is full of pages that are technically online but socially dead. They still resolve. They still render. They may even rank well in search. But they do not give any signal that a person is still tending the garden. A changelog is one of the simplest ways to say: yes, somebody is here, and yes, the lights are on.

It also lowers the pressure of publishing.

One reason people stop updating personal sites is that every update starts to feel like it needs to be a Real Thing. A polished essay. A full project launch. A complete redesign. That is exhausting. If the only valid move is “publish something substantial,” the website slowly turns into homework.

A changelog creates a middle ground. You can ship smaller truths.

Maybe you rewrote your about page because the old version sounded like it was drafted by a very nervous LinkedIn ghostwriter. That is worth logging. Maybe you added alt text to old posts. Worth logging. Maybe you finally removed the “currently reading” widget that had been claiming you were halfway through the same book since February. Extremely worth logging.

There is a nice cultural precedent for this too. The old web was full of little status signals: update stamps, journal entries, blogroll tweaks, hand-edited homepage notes, and gloriously specific side pages. It was messier, sure, but it was also easier to feel the person behind the page. Today, many personal sites are visually cleaner and emotionally quieter. Changelogs can bring back some of that texture without requiring anyone to resurrect a blinking GIF addiction.

They are also useful for readers.

If I land on somebody's site and see a concise changelog, I learn what they care about by what they keep improving. Did they add a reading log? Refactor project writeups? Start posting sketches instead of essays? Build a tiny tool and immediately document the rough edges? That tells me more than a perfectly polished homepage often does.

It also gives repeat visitors a reason to check in. Software products figured this out a long time ago. People like visible motion. Even tiny changes help create momentum. GitHub itself leans heavily on activity as a signal because activity is interesting. A quiet site may still be excellent, but an active site invites a relationship.

If you want a model, I like the spirit of the release notes culture around small software tools and indie projects, where updates are short, specific, and not embarrassed to be incremental. And if you want a more philosophical nudge, the excellent essay collection around the [small web](https://benhoyt.com/writings/the-small-web-is-beautiful/) makes a strong case for websites that feel personal instead of platform-shaped.

The best part is that a changelog can stay lightweight.

It does not need a custom database, filtering UI, or a fancy timeline animation that accidentally takes down your Lighthouse score. A Markdown page is enough. Reverse chronological bullets are enough. Dates, a sentence, maybe a link. Done.

In fact, the lower-tech it is, the better. Personal websites should resist the urge to become tiny enterprises. The whole charm is that they are allowed to be idiosyncratic. A good changelog can say, very plainly, “I made this weird little corner of the internet slightly better today.”

That is a satisfying sentence. More sites should get to say it.

I suspect changelogs would also improve the sites themselves. Once you are keeping a public record of changes, even a humble one, you start noticing what deserves movement. Broken page? Fix it. Dead project link? Clean it up. Draft worth posting? Post it. Changelogs reward maintenance, and maintenance is underrated. The web does not only need more new things. It needs more cared-for things.

So yes, I am arguing for one more page on your already modest website. But this one earns its keep. It makes the site feel alive, gives you permission to publish smaller updates, and reminds readers that the internet is still made out of people doing work in public.

Also, if nothing else, it creates a dignified place to admit that you changed your favicon three times in one afternoon.

Which, to be clear, is exactly the kind of honesty the web deserves.
