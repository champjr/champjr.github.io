---
title: "The Internet Needs More Side Doors"
pubDate: 2026-05-10
description: "A short case for wandering the web through side entrances instead of always arriving through the same giant front gates."
tags: [daily, internet, discovery, web, writing]
---

A lot of the web now feels like entering a city through the airport.

Technically, you arrived. Practically, you are not *there* yet.

You passed through a huge standardized funnel, saw the same chain restaurants, and ended up in a polished transit corridor designed to move large numbers of people in predictable ways. Efficient, sure. Memorable, not really.

That is how the modern internet can feel when every journey starts from the same few giant platforms.

Search still matters. Social feeds still matter. Recommendation engines are often genuinely useful. I am not trying to cosplay as a noble digital woodsman who discovers every website by candlelight and hand-drawn map. Sometimes you just need the answer to a question, and a search box is a miracle.

But I do think the internet gets more interesting when we use more side doors.

By side doors, I mean all the lower-glamour ways of finding things online: blogrolls, personal links pages, tiny newsletters, forum signatures, project READMEs, footnotes, indie directories, the “uses” pages people keep on their sites, old-school hyperlinks embedded in essays, even the occasional delightfully obsessive Wikipedia rabbit hole.

These paths are worse at scale and better at texture.

A recommendation algorithm is optimized to predict what has a high chance of keeping you engaged. A side door is usually just one person quietly saying, “This is interesting, you should look.” That difference matters. One is statistical. The other is editorial.

And yes, editorial taste can be weird, biased, and gloriously specific. Good.

I want more of that.

One reason the early web felt so alive, at least in memory, is that it was full of human breadcrumbs. You did not just consume pages. You moved through people’s attention. This homepage linked to that strange art project. That project linked to a niche tool. The tool’s documentation linked to a researcher’s paper. The paper linked to a lab page that looked like it had not changed since 2003, which honestly increased my trust slightly.

The route itself taught you something. You could tell *why* a thing had been linked, not just that it was broadly popular.

This is part of why I still like the [IndieWeb](https://indieweb.org/) ethos, even if not everyone wants to run their own tiny corner of the web with handcrafted markup and feelings. The bigger idea is what matters: personal sites as living places, not just brand containers. Places that point outward. Places with edges. Places where a recommendation feels like a host walking you into another room.

The giant feeds are very good at giving you more of what already works. They are less good at giving you the slightly off-angle thing that rearranges your taste.

That is not because they are evil in some cartoon way. It is because at scale, predictability wins. The system has little reason to say, “Here is a slightly awkward essay from a person with 42 readers, but it contains a sentence you will think about for a month.” That kind of recommendation is expensive, fragile, and not obviously monetizable. Which is precisely why humans should keep doing it for one another.

There is also a different emotional tempo in side-door browsing. You are less likely to feel processed. Less likely to feel that every click is being fed back into a machine whose main job is to sharpen your future clicks into a more legible product. Sometimes you are just wandering. Sometimes you are following a hunch. Sometimes you end up on a page that is useful in a way no engagement model would have predicted.

I think a lot of people miss this feeling without having a clean name for it.

They say the internet feels flatter now, or more repetitive, or strangely over-optimized. They are not imagining it. When discovery is concentrated into a few massive systems, the web starts to inherit their incentives. Everything wants to be instantly legible, clip-worthy, searchable, rankable, shareable, and preferably compressed into a format that survives the feed.

But many interesting things are bad at making a first impression.

Some ideas need three paragraphs before they become themselves. Some websites are charmingly inconvenient. Some people have genuine taste but terrible thumbnails. Some projects are too niche to travel well through mainstream distribution, which is often a sign that they may be exactly worth visiting.

This is why I have a soft spot for the awkward infrastructure of personal recommendation. Send me the weird link with one sentence of context. Keep a page of bookmarks. Put a “related” section at the bottom of your post that reflects your actual brain instead of SEO superstition. Link generously. Link specifically. Build a tiny trail for the next curious person.

The funny part is that none of this requires nostalgia for a golden age that never quite existed. The web was always messy. It was always full of junk, abandoned pages, and people with far too much confidence in animated GIFs. That was part of the charm too.

What I want back is not the old internet, exactly. It is the sense that the web can still be navigated by human taste instead of mostly by industrial routing.

A healthy city has main roads, sure. It also has alleyways, corner shops, side entrances, hidden courtyards, and the café you only find because someone you trust says, “No, not that place, go two blocks over and turn left.”

The internet deserves that kind of urbanism.

So here is my tiny plea: make side doors.

Link to the obscure thing. Keep the blogroll. Publish the reading list. Put your favorite tools on a plain little page. Write the post that points to three other posts. Be a slightly opinionated guide instead of a passive node in somebody else’s distribution system.

The front gates will take care of themselves.

What the web needs more of is people leaving the side entrance unlocked.
