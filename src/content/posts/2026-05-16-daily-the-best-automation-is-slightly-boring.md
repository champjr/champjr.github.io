---
title: "The Best Automation Is Slightly Boring"
pubDate: 2026-05-16
description: "A case for automations that quietly reduce friction instead of performing little demo reels every time they run."
tags: [daily, automation, workflow, software, product]
---

People love flashy automation demos.

A thing wiggles across the screen, three apps talk to each other, a dashboard lights up, and suddenly it looks like the future has arrived wearing a tidy little webhook. I get the appeal. Automation is fun partly because it lets us feel briefly smarter than our own chores.

But the longer I use software, the more I think the best automation is usually a little boring.

Not bad boring. Good boring. The kind of boring that disappears into the floorboards of your day.

The glamorous version of automation gets all the attention because it is easy to show. “When this happens, send that, summarize this, update that sheet, alert this channel, and generate a color-coded status emoji.” Lovely. Very cinematic. It looks great in a thread and even better in a keynote.

The useful version is less dramatic.

The useful version quietly renames your downloads. It files a receipt where you can actually find it later. It adds a calendar buffer so your schedule stops pretending teleportation is real. It closes a tiny gap that you used to step over twenty times a week.

That kind of automation rarely goes viral because it does not look ambitious enough. It does not make bystanders say “whoa.” It makes one specific person, often the person who built it, stop getting annoyed every afternoon at 3:17.

I think that is a much better success metric.

A lot of automation goes wrong because it is designed as a performance before it is designed as infrastructure. People try to automate a whole business process, a whole creative pipeline, or a whole personal operating system all at once. They build a chain so elaborate that it feels less like saving time and more like adopting a very high-maintenance pet.

Then something tiny breaks. An API field changes. A file arrives with a weird name. A service rate-limits you. One step assumes the world is tidy, and the world, with its usual cheerful disrespect for assumptions, refuses to cooperate.

Now instead of one manual task, you have a haunted Rube Goldberg machine and a new hobby in incident response.

This is why I have become more opinionated about the right unit of automation.

The right unit is often not “replace the human.” It is “remove one repeatable paper cut.”

That sounds less grand, but it scales better. Small automations are easier to trust, easier to debug, and easier to keep alive after the original burst of enthusiasm fades. They also fail more gracefully. If a script stops sorting screenshots into folders, annoying. If your giant life-dashboard stops orchestrating your calendar, tasks, email, and notes in one synchronized ballet, you have invented an avoidable species of stress.

There is a design lesson hiding in that.

Good automation should reduce the number of things you have to remember, not increase them. If I need to remember six caveats, three fallback paths, and which moon phase causes the workflow to duplicate rows, then the automation is not helping me. It is just relocating complexity to a place where it is harder to see.

The best automations have a few nice qualities in common:

- they do something narrow and obvious
- they save a task you genuinely repeat
- they are easy to inspect when they fail
- they degrade into a manual process that is still understandable

This is part of why simple Unix-style tools and boring scripts keep surviving every wave of software fashion. A tiny shell script that resizes images or archives logs may not be sexy, but it is legible. You can open it, read it, tweak it, and generally avoid entering a blood pact with an opaque workflow builder. There is a reason people still reach for plain cron, shell, and small task runners decades later. Boring tools tend to have excellent survival rates.

I also think people underestimate the emotional value of modest automation.

A huge automation system often asks for trust up front. “Hand me your process and I will optimize your life.” That is a lot to ask. A small automation earns trust incrementally. “Hey, I noticed you always do this annoying thing. Want me to take that part?” That feels polite. It feels believable.

And believable systems get used.

This is one reason I like the idea of automation as furniture rather than fireworks. Good furniture does not demand applause. It supports the activity. You notice it most when it is missing, broken, or weirdly shaped. A sturdy shelf is not exciting, but I would rather own a sturdy shelf than a chandelier that occasionally sends my notebooks into the ceiling.

There is a broader product point here too. A lot of software companies sell automation as maximalist transformation because transformation is easier to market than relief. Relief sounds small. Relief sounds incremental. Relief sounds like fewer clicks and less context switching and a slightly calmer Tuesday.

But honestly, that is what many people are buying.

Not an autonomous empire of agents.

Just a calmer Tuesday.

If you want a good test for whether an automation is worth keeping, try this: if it disappeared tomorrow, would you miss the outcome or just the idea of being the kind of person who has cool automations?

That question is a little rude, which is why it is useful.

Some automations exist mostly to flatter their owner. They produce screenshots, not stability. Others become part of the texture of your day so completely that you stop thinking about them. Those are the keepers.

This is also why I think one of the best places to look for automation opportunities is not in your grand plans but in your recurring sighs. What do you do over and over with mild resentment? What makes you mutter “why am I still doing this by hand?” What tiny ritual keeps stealing attention from better work?

Start there.

If you need inspiration, the old [IFTTT](https://ifttt.com/explore) gallery remains a good reminder that useful automation often starts with very ordinary triggers and very ordinary outcomes. Not revolutionary, just relieving.

The future probably will contain some impressively smart automations. A few already do feel magical. But I suspect the lasting wins will continue to be the unspectacular ones, the little systems that quietly absorb friction and then get out of the way.

In other words, the best automation is often the one nobody thinks to brag about.

Including, after a while, you.
