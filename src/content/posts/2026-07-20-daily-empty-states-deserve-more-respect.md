---
title: "Empty States Deserve More Respect"
pubDate: 2026-07-20
description: "The blank moments in software do a surprising amount of teaching, reassurance, and tone-setting."
tags: [daily, product, software, ux, design]
---

Some of the most important writing in software happens when there is nothing there.

No tasks yet. No search results. No saved articles. No projects. No files. No messages. Just a blank patch of interface trying not to feel like a dead end.

That is the empty state, and I think it deserves way more respect than it usually gets.

Product teams pour a lot of energy into the busy state. They design dashboards full of charts, lists full of content, feeds full of movement, editors full of features, workflows full of buttons. That makes sense. It is easier to imagine the product at full power.

But most people do not meet software at full power.

They meet it on day one.

Or after a cleanup.

Or in a new workspace.

Or right after searching for something that does not exist.

In other words, they meet it in the quiet.

And the quiet can either feel inviting or mildly accusatory.

We have all seen the bad version. A giant blank card. Maybe a lonely icon floating in space. A sentence like “Nothing here yet.” Thanks, detective work complete. Sometimes there is a button, sometimes not. Sometimes the app seems almost offended that you have not already populated it with a meaningful life.

Good empty states do something better. They answer one of three questions clearly.

What is this place for?

What should I do next?

Is everything okay?

That last one matters more than people think. Not every empty state means “you should create something.” Sometimes it means “your inbox is cleared,” “there are no alerts,” or “nothing matched your filters.” Those are very different emotions. One is an invitation. One is a clean bill of health. One is a nudge to adjust your query before you start doubting reality.

When software fails to distinguish those moods, it creates tiny avoidable confusion. The user wonders whether the app is empty, broken, syncing, or silently judging them.

This is why I think empty states are less like decoration and more like interface ethics. They are where a product reveals whether it understands the human on the other side.

A good empty state does not just fill space. It orients.

It says, for example: this area holds your saved searches. Or: no invoices are overdue, nice work. Or: we did not find anything for that term, but here are a few ways to broaden it. Tiny difference on paper, huge difference in lived experience.

I also think empty states are one of the clearest places where product tone can either help or absolutely overcook it.

A little personality is nice. A lightly funny line can be charming. But there is a thin line between friendly and trying way too hard while I am simply looking for a file. If I search a knowledge base and get zero results, I do not need a cartoon raccoon telling me to “go on an adventure.” I need the app to suggest a spelling fix, a broader filter, or a nearby category. The raccoon can sit this one out.

This is the part that makes empty states interesting to me. They are not only about copy. They are about product judgment.

What action do you highlight?

What examples do you show?

Do you provide starter content?

Do you explain the shape of the feature without forcing a tutorial?

Do you make the blank page feel like possibility instead of absence?

Some of the best products use empty states as a soft first lesson. Not a modal. Not a video. Not a twelve-step checklist with the energy of a hostage negotiation. Just a well-placed example, a clear button, and a sentence that quietly teaches the mental model.

This matters because users are usually building confidence before they are building anything else.

An empty state can say: you are in the right place, here is how this works, start here.

That is a lot of value from very little surface area.

It is also where restraint pays off. The best empty states do not try to solve everything at once. They avoid turning blank space into a marketing zone stuffed with upsells, banners, webinars, and feature announcements. If the interface is empty, that does not automatically mean it should become Times Square.

Sometimes the kindest thing software can do is be clear and get out of the way.

I suspect empty states are underrated because they are hard to celebrate. Nobody posts screenshots saying, “wow, this zero-results page really understood me.” But when they are done badly, people feel the friction immediately. The product feels colder. More confusing. Less trustworthy. Like it has only been designed for the moment after you already know how to use it.

That is backwards.

The edges matter. The first-use moments matter. The cleared-out moments matter. The search-that-found-nothing moment matters.

Software should know how to behave when there is something to show.

But really good software also knows how to behave when there is not.
