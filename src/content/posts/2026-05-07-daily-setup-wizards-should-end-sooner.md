---
title: "Setup Wizards Should End Sooner"
pubDate: 2026-05-07
description: "Why too much onboarding often reveals a product that is trying to explain around its own complexity."
tags: [daily, product, onboarding, software, design]
---

There is a point in many software products where the setup flow stops being helpful and starts feeling like a hostage note from the product team.

Pick your workspace name. Invite your teammates. Choose your color theme. Connect three integrations. Define success. Select your use case. Watch a cheerful animation about collaboration. Answer a question that no one can answer yet, like whether your organization prefers “impact tracking” or “goal alignment.”

Friend, I just wanted to try the app.

I think setup wizards should end sooner than most of them do.

Not because onboarding is bad. Good onboarding is generous. It reduces uncertainty, trims obvious mistakes, and gives people a clean first win. But a lot of software does something sneakier. It turns onboarding into a ceremonial tunnel the user has to crawl through before they are allowed to touch the actual product.

That is usually a smell.

When a tool needs twelve screens to explain how to begin, one possibility is that the team cares deeply about user success. Another possibility, and in my experience a common one, is that the product is trying to explain around complexity it has not really tamed.

A short setup flow says, “We know what you’re here to do.”

A long one often says, “We built a platform and now we need you to help us decide what it is.”

I am a big believer in what you might call the lobby test. When you enter a new building, the lobby should orient you without making you sign up for a guided tour, declare your strategic priorities, and choose a preferred carpet taxonomy. You need to know where to go next, not absorb the full philosophy of the architecture.

Software should work the same way.

The first few minutes with a product should answer a simple question: can this help me do the thing I came here to do? Not someday, not after a configuration pilgrimage, but now, in a small real way.

This is one reason I like products that let you start with a sample project, a fake dataset, a tiny sandbox, or even a decent default document. Show me the shape of success before you ask me to manufacture it from scratch. The blank page is not always respectful. Sometimes it is just lazy.

There is a useful concept from human-computer interaction called progressive disclosure, where advanced options are revealed as users need them rather than dumped on them all at once. The Nielsen Norman Group has a good explanation of the pattern [here](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/). The idea is pleasantly unglamorous: do not make people carry cognitive weight before it becomes relevant.

A surprising amount of modern onboarding violates that rule on purpose.

Why? Because the setup flow is doing too many jobs.

It is trying to educate the user. It is trying to personalize the product. It is trying to collect sales data. It is trying to push team invites because the growth dashboard likes that. It is trying to preload habits that might improve retention. It is trying to reassure investors, somehow spiritually, that this software is enterprise-grade.

That is a lot to ask of a few little “Continue” buttons.

The result is that onboarding often becomes a political compromise disguised as a friendly welcome. Nobody wants to remove their favorite question, so the wizard grows another page. Nobody wants to delay a monetization prompt, so the user gets asked to commit before they have experienced value. Nobody wants to hide a powerful feature, so the newcomer meets the entire cast on page one.

This is how you get products that feel nervous.

Calm software does less at the door.

It trusts that some understanding can happen *after* use begins. It knows that the best explanation for a tool is often the tool itself, especially if the first task is concrete enough. It treats onboarding as orientation, not initiation.

That distinction matters because people do not evaluate software in a vacuum. They are comparing your setup flow with all the other setup flows they have survived this month. Every extra click is not just local friction. It lands in a broader emotional economy of “please do not make me learn another system before lunch.”

I think product teams sometimes underestimate how much goodwill is created by quick usefulness. If I can accomplish one real thing in the first three minutes, I become dramatically more patient with the next ten minutes. If I am still naming categories and choosing templates after minute seven, my faith starts leaving my body in a visible vapor.

There is also a strategic angle here. A long onboarding flow tends to attract internal justification. Once it exists, everyone can point to it as proof that the product is “guiding” users. But guidance that happens before contact with reality is fragile. Users answer setup questions differently when they actually understand the work. Early choices made in ignorance are often just decorative guesses.

So the product ends up with worse data, the user ends up slightly annoyed, and everyone politely pretends the wizard was necessary because it had rounded corners and a progress bar.

My bias is simple: if a question can wait until after the user has experienced value, it probably should. If a feature only matters to advanced users, hide it until they become advanced users. If a setup step exists mainly because another team benefits from the information, at least be honest that the request is serving the business, not the beginner.

And if the product still cannot function without a long series of up-front decisions, that may be real. Some complex tools really do need careful configuration. But then the design task is not to make the wizard prettier. It is to ask whether the product can offer a smaller first room.

A sandbox. A template. A default path. A single-player mode before the multiplayer sermon.

Let me in. Let me touch the thing. Let me earn the complicated part.

The best setup wizard is not the one with the cutest illustrations or the smoothest step transitions.

It is the one that knows when to stop talking.
