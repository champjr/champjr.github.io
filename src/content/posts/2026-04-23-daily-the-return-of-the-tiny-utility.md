---
title: "The Return of the Tiny Utility"
pubDate: 2026-04-23
description: "Why small, single-purpose tools keep winning a permanent place in modern workflows."
tags: [daily, tools, software, workflow, product]
---

There is a kind of software I keep rooting for: the tiny utility.

Not the giant platform with a manifesto, a billing portal, a collaboration suite, and a roadmap that reads like it wants to become your operating system. I mean the little tool that does one job cleanly, maybe two if it is feeling ambitious, and then gets out of the way like a professional.

For a while, it seemed like software culture wanted to grow past these tools. Everything had to become a platform. A note app needed tasks, publishing, AI summaries, team spaces, and probably a second sidebar for spiritual reasons. A screenshot tool could not merely capture screens. It needed cloud sync, async feedback, comments, version history, and a startup-style landing page explaining how visual collaboration unlocks alignment.

Meanwhile, plenty of people still just wanted to crop an image and move on with their lives.

I think the tiny utility is coming back, or maybe it never actually left. It just got drowned out by louder software.

The reason is not nostalgia. It is that small tools match the shape of real work surprisingly well.

Most tasks in a day are not grand strategic flows. They are little moments of intent. Resize this file. Rename this batch. Convert this audio clip. Search this folder. Diff these two texts. Strip metadata. Extract frames. Compress the PDF that somehow became larger than the thing it was describing. You do not need a digital empire for these jobs. You need a sharp knife.

That is the appeal.

Tiny utilities tend to have a few advantages that bigger software keeps forgetting.

First, they respect momentum. You open them, do the thing, and continue with the rest of your day. There is no onboarding ceremony. No tour. No motivational empty state gently suggesting that maybe you would enjoy inviting your team. Good utility software assumes you already have a reason for being there. That alone makes it feel more adult.

Second, they keep cognitive load low. One of the hidden costs of modern software is not just time, it is context. Every extra pane, mode, and feature asks you to load a slightly larger mental model. Tiny tools are easier on the brain because they are legible. Their whole deal fits in your head. You can build trust with them quickly.

Third, they compose well. A small tool does not need to own your entire workflow to be valuable. In fact, it is often better when it does not. One utility can hand off to another. A shell command can feed a script, which can generate a file, which can get cleaned up by another tiny program. This is one reason command-line culture keeps producing durable tools. Good small tools are easy to combine.

The classic Unix idea of “do one thing well” still holds up better than a lot of product strategy decks. It is not a law of nature, but it is an excellent bias. The original Unix philosophy is worth revisiting partly because it is so unfashionable in a time of maximalist apps. The old line from Doug McIlroy about writing programs that work together still feels very current. The short version lives here: [The Unix Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix_philosophy).

Of course, tiny utilities are not automatically good. Some of them are cursed in very specific ways.

A bad small tool can be obscure, under-documented, visually hostile, or abandoned by a developer who last touched it during an era when skeuomorphic leather was considered sophisticated. Small is not the same thing as thoughtful. There is a difference between focused software and software that simply stopped trying.

But when a tiny utility is good, it often creates a special kind of loyalty. People recommend these tools with the energy usually reserved for neighborhood restaurants or weirdly dependable pocket flashlights. They do this because the tool solved a recurring annoyance without turning itself into a lifestyle.

That restraint matters.

I also think small utilities benefit from a more honest relationship with value. Their pitch is clear. They save time, reduce friction, or remove drudgery. They are not promising transformation. They are promising relief. And honestly, relief is underrated. A lot of the best software does not change your identity. It just quietly removes one stupid problem from your week.

This may also be why local-first and command-line tools keep finding devoted audiences. They often preserve the virtues of the tiny utility: fast startup, clear scope, durable files, minimal ceremony, and the comforting sense that you are still in charge. No one enjoys discovering that their humble image converter now requires an account because it has “reimagined creativity through connected experiences.”

There is room for big software, obviously. Some jobs are genuinely big. Teams need shared systems. Companies need platforms. Complex domains need depth. I am not arguing that every product should become a single binary with a monochrome icon and a README written by a wizard.

I am arguing for remembering that software can be modest.

A modest tool is not lesser software. It is often better software, because it knows what it is for.

That is a quality I trust more and more.

In a world where every app seems tempted to sprawl, the tiny utility feels almost radical. It solves one problem, does not waste your time, and leaves before it can develop opinions about your workflow.

Beautiful behavior, really.
